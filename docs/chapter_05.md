# Chapter 5: Normal Variants and Benign Findings

## Overview and UK Context

The ability to confidently recognise normal variants and benign breast findings is one of the most practically important skills in breast radiology. Get it right and you reduce unnecessary recalls, spare patients avoidable biopsies, and protect the positive predictive value of the screening programme. Get it wrong in the other direction and you miss cancers hiding among everyday findings.

In the UK, this skill operates within two distinct contexts:

**The NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP)** invites women aged 50–70 for two-view mammography (craniocaudal and mediolateral oblique) every three years, with some units extending outside this range. Double reading with arbitration is standard. The critical point for a trainee is that the vast majority of screen-detected abnormalities turn out to be benign — the system is designed to be sensitive, so specificity falls to you, the reader.

**Symptomatic triple assessment** integrates clinical examination (P score), imaging (M for mammography, U for ultrasound, with tomosynthesis and MRI as adjuncts), and tissue diagnosis (B score) using the UK five-point scoring system. Understanding how each modality contributes to the final assessment, and when scores should escalate to biopsy, is the practical framework for this chapter.

A clear grasp of the scoring system is essential before proceeding:

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| P/M/U 1 | Normal |
| P/M/U 2 | Benign |
| P/M/U 3 | Indeterminate / possibly benign |
| P/M/U 4 | Suspicious |
| P/M/U 5 | Malignant |
| B1 | Normal tissue |
| B2 | Benign |
| B3 | Uncertain malignant potential |
| B4 | Suspicious |
| B5 | Malignant |

M2/U2 findings do not require recall in the screening context. M3/U3 findings are recalled for further assessment and often biopsy. Any imaging–pathology discordance must be resolved at MDT.

## Foundational Principles

Before cataloguing specific entities, it is worth anchoring your pattern recognition in the underlying biology. Isolated facts are forgotten; principles stick.

**Form follows histology.** Fat is radiolucent; fibrous and epithelial tissue attenuates X-rays and appears dense. Benign processes — whether cysts, fibroadenomas or lipomas — tend to expand and compress surrounding tissue, producing smooth, pushing margins. Malignancy incites a desmoplastic stromal reaction that tethers and infiltrates, producing spiculation and architectural distortion. This is why margins are the single most discriminating mammographic feature.

**Calcifications encode biology in their morphology and distribution.** Coarse, dystrophic, rim, vascular and milk-of-calcium calcifications reflect benign processes such as involution, fat necrosis and calcium precipitation. Fine pleomorphic, linear or branching calcifications in a segmental distribution reflect intraductal disease — typically DCIS — producing calcium within necrotic tumour or secretions in the ductal tree. When you see calcifications, ask yourself: does their morphology suggest organised deposition (benign) or chaotic intraductal proliferation (malignant)? Does their distribution follow a ductal-lobular anatomy (segmental/linear, concerning) or scatter diffusely (usually benign)?

**Distribution tells a biological story.** Diffuse, bilateral, or scattered changes almost always reflect benign background processes such as fibrocystic change or vascular disease. Focal, segmental, or linear changes aligned with a ductal tree demand explanation. A finding confined to one quadrant in a single breast warrants more scrutiny than its bilateral mirror image.

**Multimodality correlation resolves most ambiguity.** Tomosynthesis distinguishes summation artefact from a genuine mass; ultrasound distinguishes cystic from solid; MRI solves problem cases and evaluates extent. You do not need MRI for most benign diagnoses, but you should know when it adds value.

**Prior imaging is the single most powerful tool you have.** A circumscribed mass stable over five years is almost certainly benign, even if its appearance is not entirely typical. Interval change demands explanation, even in an otherwise benign-looking lesion.

## Normal Anatomy and Variants That Mimic Disease

### Parenchymal Pattern and Breast Density

Fibroglandular tissue involutes with age, decreasing parity, and post-menopausally (unless hormone replacement therapy is used). High breast density has two practical consequences: it reduces mammographic sensitivity by obscuring lesions, and it increases the rate of summation artefacts — apparent densities caused by overlapping normal tissue superimposing on a single two-dimensional projection.

Summation density is the commonest cause of recall in screening. The key question is always: does this finding exist in three dimensions? Orthogonal views, spot compression, rolled views, and — most usefully — digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) resolve the majority of summation artefacts. On DBT, a real mass has borders that persist across multiple adjacent reconstructed slices; superimposed tissue disappears on adjacent planes.

A one-view focal asymmetry that disperses on spot compression, has no correlate on the orthogonal view, and is negative on targeted ultrasound is almost always summation — score M2. If it persists on additional views but lacks suspicious features, score M3 and assess with targeted ultrasound and tomosynthesis; if still indeterminate, discuss at MDT.

### The Nipple, Areola, and Subareolar Region

Normal nipples vary in projection and may be congenitally inverted bilaterally. Long-standing bilateral inversion is a normal variant. New, unilateral inversion — especially combined with subareolar distortion or skin thickening — is a red flag and warrants full triple assessment.

Montgomery glands, periareolar sebaceous cysts, and mild ductal ectasia produce palpable and visible areolar nodularity that can alarm patients and clinicians. Recognise these as benign at ultrasound: subdermal, well-circumscribed, avascular, without suspicious features.

*Practical tip:* If the position of the nipple on the mammogram is uncertain — particularly on the MLO view — apply a radiopaque skin marker and repeat. This prevents a well-projecting nipple being recalled as a subareolar mass.

### Axillary Tail and Accessory Tissue

The axillary tail of Spence extends into the axilla and can be asymmetrical and prominent. True accessory breast tissue — distinct from the axillary tail — can occur anywhere along the embryological milk line (most often axilla or inframammary fold). It behaves like normal breast parenchyma: it responds to hormonal stimulation, can develop fibrocystic change, and very rarely can harbour malignancy. Recognise its characteristic location and parenchymal texture.

**Intramammary lymph nodes (IMLNs)** are common, particularly in the upper outer quadrant. Their hallmark is a reniform shape with a central lucent fatty hilum on mammography and a thin cortex with preserved echogenic hilum on ultrasound. Size is a poor criterion in isolation; morphology matters more. A node ≤10–12 mm with a preserved fatty hilum is M2/U2. Loss of hilum, cortical thickening >3 mm, or a round shape warrants further assessment regardless of size.

### Chest Wall Muscles and Anatomical Structures

Pectoralis major and serratus anterior fibres can be partially imaged and mimic a mass. The MLO projection should include the pectoral muscle down to the posterior nipple line; a triangular anterior muscle edge is a normal landmark, not a finding.

The **sternalis muscle** is an incidental anatomical variant — a vertical parasternal sheet of muscle that appears as a stable, anterior medial density, isodense with muscle. It is worth recognising because it can prompt unnecessary recall if unfamiliar.

Tortuous vessels and vascular calcifications are common, particularly in older women and those with diabetes. Vascular calcification follows the sinuous course of arteries with the classic tram-track parallel pattern — not to be confused with the linear branching calcifications of intraductal malignancy, which follow the ductal tree rather than vessel walls.

### Skin Lesions and Dermal Structures

Naevi, seborrhoeic keratoses, and neurofibromas can project onto the breast parenchyma and appear as circumscribed masses. Features suggesting a dermal origin include: superficial location, visibility on the skin surface at clinical examination, a "halo" of air between the lesion and parenchyma on the mammogram, and movement with the skin rather than the underlying tissue. A tangential view with a skin marker placed on the lesion confirms its dermal location and avoids unnecessary biopsy.

Skin folds — particularly in large or ptotic breasts — create linear edge densities that can mimic architectural distortion. Repositioning resolves the problem.

**The practical rule:** always mark palpable lumps, skin lesions, scars, and drains/clips before imaging. A few seconds with a marker prevents unnecessary recalls.

### Post-Surgical and Post-Procedural Change

Post-lumpectomy architectural distortion converging on a biopsy clip or surgical scar is expected, and typically softens and reduces over two to three years. New spiculation, an increase in opacity, or interval change near a surgical scar requires careful comparison with priors and, if in doubt, biopsy.

Reduction mammoplasty creates bilateral symmetrical scars with superior parenchymal redistribution and dystrophic calcifications — recognise it as a pattern rather than recalling everything.

For breast implants, remember that standard two-view mammography may be supplemented by Eklund (implant-displaced) views to maximise parenchymal assessment. Extracapsular silicone produces the characteristic **"snowstorm" artefact** on ultrasound. Intracapsular rupture is best assessed with MRI, where the collapsed shell produces the **"linguine sign"** — folded hypointense lines within the silicone.

## Technical and Interpretive Pitfalls

These are sources of avoidable recalls and errors in both directions.

**Summation density** is covered above — the commonest false positive in screening. Use tomosynthesis, spot compression, and priors to resolve.

**Deodorant and powder** — particularly talcum powder — create fine cutaneous densities that can closely mimic calcifications. If you see multiple tiny densities clustered near the skin in the axillary region or upper breast, ask the radiographer to clean and re-image.

**External artefacts** (ECG leads, jewellery, oxygen tubing, hair braids) create geometric, sharp-edged densities that are obviously extrinsic if you think to look. They should not reach MDT.

**Positioning artefacts** — inadequate pectoral muscle on MLO, truncated posterior tissue on CC, or the nipple not in profile — can obscure posterior lesions or create artificial-appearing densities. Part of reading a mammogram is assessing image quality before interpreting the content.

## Cysts and Duct-Related Benign Findings

### Simple Cyst

Simple cysts arise from distended terminal duct lobular units (TDLUs) and are hormonally responsive — common in the perimenopausal period and in women on HRT. On mammography they appear as round or oval, circumscribed, equal or slightly low density masses — often invisible in dense tissue. On ultrasound the diagnosis is definitive when all four criteria are met: **anechoic content, imperceptible wall, posterior acoustic enhancement, no internal vascularity**. Score U2; no biopsy required.

Aspirate only if the cyst is symptomatic, tense, or if there is genuine diagnostic uncertainty. Aspirated fluid need not be sent for cytology unless it is bloodstained.

*Pitfall:* A simple cyst with peripheral calcification can appear dense or irregular on mammography. Ultrasound resolves this — the cystic nature is apparent sonographically even when mammography is misleading.

### Complicated Cysts and Clusters of Microcysts

**Complicated cysts** contain low-level internal echoes — representing proteinaceous fluid, haemorrhage, or cellular debris — but lack a solid vascular component. They may show a fluid-debris level. With typical features they are U2–U3 depending on the degree of confidence and clinical context. If a complicated cyst convincingly explains a mammographic mass and has no suspicious features, reassurance or short-term follow-up (six months) is reasonable. If in doubt, biopsy.

**Clusters of microcysts** — grouped tiny anechoic foci with thin septations and no internal vascularity — are an important benign pattern. They commonly underlie grouped amorphous or punctate calcifications on mammography (representing milk-of-calcium or apocrine secretions within the microcysts). If the ultrasound cluster is concordant with the mammographic finding and is typical, this is a B2/U2 diagnosis at biopsy or, if appearances are classic, can be managed with surveillance after MDT discussion. However, be cautious: amorphous grouped calcifications have a significant DCIS overlap, and many UK units proceed to stereotactic biopsy for this group.

**The critical distinction:** a complicated cyst is not the same as a complex cystic-solid mass. Any mass with a solid vascular component requires biopsy.

### Ductal Ectasia and Periductal Change

Duct ectasia — dilation of major ducts with inspissated secretions and periductal inflammation — is common peri- and post-menopausally. On mammography, look for tubular retroareolar densities and rod-like, coarse calcifications radiating from the nipple (secretory calcifications). On ultrasound, dilated ducts with echogenic debris are characteristic; wall thickening may be present but there should be no intraductal vascular soft tissue mass.

Management is conservative in most cases. Investigate unilateral bloody or serous discharge, or an intraductal vascular mass on ultrasound, to exclude an intraductal papilloma.

### Intraductal Papilloma

A central solitary papilloma — the most common cause of unilateral nipple discharge, often blood-stained or serous — may be occult on mammography but is identifiable on ultrasound as an intraluminal soft tissue mass within a dilated duct, often with a vascular stalk on Doppler. Because of a non-negligible upgrade rate (especially when atypia is present at core biopsy, or in peripheral papillomas which have a higher association with atypia and malignancy), standard practice in the UK is to offer tissue diagnosis. Management after B2 core biopsy — vacuum excision versus imaging surveillance — varies between units and should follow local MDT policy.

## Fibroepithelial and Stromal Benign Masses

### Fibroadenoma

The commonest solid benign breast mass, most prevalent in women aged 15–40 but encountered at any age. The mixed stromal and epithelial proliferation grows by expansion, producing the characteristically smooth, pushing margin.

- **Mammography:** Oval or round, circumscribed, equal or low density. Coarse "popcorn" calcifications within an involuting fibroadenoma are essentially pathognomonic — do not biopsy these.
- **Ultrasound:** Oval, parallel orientation (wider than tall), circumscribed, homogeneously hypoechoic or isoechoic with gentle macrolobulations, minimal posterior enhancement, avascular or with peripheral flow only. Score U2.
- **Management:** Classic appearance in a young woman (under 40) — discharge with M2/U2. However, exercise caution with new circumscribed masses in women over 40 where the differential includes circumscribed carcinoma (mucinous, medullary-like, metaplastic) or phyllodes tumour. Biopsy if: age >40 with a new lesion, rapid growth, atypical imaging features, or clinical concern (P3+).

**Phyllodes tumour** shares many imaging features with fibroadenoma but tends to be larger, faster-growing, and may show internal cleft-like spaces on ultrasound. Biopsy is recommended for masses ≥3 cm, those showing interval growth, or those with atypical features. Note that core biopsy distinguishes benign phyllodes from fibroadenoma less reliably than excision.

### Hamartoma (Fibroadenolipoma)

The hamartoma's imaging appearance is essentially diagnostic: a well-circumscribed mass with a thin pseudo-capsule containing a mixture of fat and fibroglandular tissue — the classic **"breast within a breast"** appearance. Both components must be identifiable. On ultrasound it is heterogeneous, compressible, and well-circumscribed. Score M2/U2 if classic; biopsy if the fat component is absent (raising concern for a soft tissue mass) or if other features are atypical.

### Lipoma

A purely fatty, well-circumscribed mass with a thin capsule. Radiolucent on mammography; homogeneously echogenic or isoechoic and compressible on ultrasound; avascular. Classic appearances need no biopsy.

### Pseudoangiomatous Stromal Hyperplasia (PASH)

PASH is a benign myofibroblastic stromal proliferation that forms slit-like pseudovascular spaces. It can be an incidental microscopic finding at biopsy (the common form) or manifest as a palpable, sometimes large, mass.

- **Imaging:** Circumscribed or partially obscured mass on mammography; oval, circumscribed, homogeneously hypoechoic, often avascular on ultrasound. Can be large and may enlarge over time.
- **Management:** B2 at core biopsy is concordant. Observation is appropriate for stable PASH; excision is considered for significant interval growth or diagnostic uncertainty. Do not be falsely reassured by an initial B2 if the lesion enlarges.

### Focal Fibrosis and Sclerosing Adenosis

Both are benign proliferative processes that can cause architectural distortion and mimic carcinoma on both mammography and ultrasound. Sclerosing adenosis in particular produces a hypoechoic area with posterior acoustic shadowing that can be indistinguishable from invasive carcinoma sonographically — and often calcifies (usually producing grouped amorphous or punctate calcifications). The diagnosis is histological: B2 at core biopsy. Imaging–pathology concordance at MDT is essential here, as these lesions are a common source of benign–malignant diagnostic uncertainty.

## Fat Necrosis and Post-Traumatic Change

Fat necrosis results from saponification and liquefaction of adipose tissue following trauma (including surgery, radiotherapy, and biopsy). Its imaging appearances evolve over time, which is important to understand:

- **Early fat necrosis** — weeks to months — can appear as a focal asymmetry, irregular mass, or area of architectural distortion. At this stage it can genuinely mimic carcinoma, particularly in a post-surgical breast.
- **Established fat necrosis** — months to years — evolves to the characteristic **oil cyst**: a radiolucent (fat-density) mass with a thin "eggshell" rim of calcification on mammography. This is diagnostic and requires no biopsy (M2/U2). On ultrasound, fat necrosis can appear as a complex cyst with echogenic internal fat, a fat–fluid level, echogenic bands, or mural nodularity without internal vascularity.
- **On MRI**, a thin smooth enhancing rim around a central fat signal is benign. An irregular, thickened, or nodular rim — particularly with associated enhancement — may warrant biopsy.

*The critical pitfall:* not all spiculated masses in a post-surgical breast are fat necrosis or scar. Look for interval change, a new mass-like opacity, or malignant-pattern calcifications. History of trauma is supportive but not exculpatory — biopsy when in doubt.

## Benign Calcifications: Morphology, Distribution, and Reasoning

Learning to classify calcifications confidently is one of the defining competencies of breast radiology. The logic is straightforward once you understand the underlying biology.

**Definitively benign calcifications — do not biopsy:**

- **Dermal (skin) calcifications:** Round or "lucent-centred" calcifications clustered near the skin; often with a "tattoo sign" visible on skin surface. Confirm on tangential view with skin marker.
- **Vascular calcifications:** Parallel tram-track linear calcifications following sinuous vascular structures. Common medially and inferiorly. Not to be confused with linear intraductal calcifications.
- **Coarse "popcorn" calcifications:** Involuting fibroadenoma. Pathognomonic when typical.
- **Rim calcifications:** Thin peripheral calcification around oil cysts or fat necrosis. Radiolucent centre confirms the diagnosis.
- **Dystrophic calcifications:** Coarse, irregular calcifications in irradiated, inflamed, or traumatised tissue. Benign in the right clinical context.
- **Secretory (plasma cell mastitis) calcifications:** Rod-like, branching, retroareolar; often bilateral; follow large ducts. Benign. *Do not confuse these with linear/branching malignant calcifications*, which are finer, more irregular, and follow a segmental distribution within the TDLU tree rather than the large central ducts.
- **Milk of calcium:** Calcium precipitating in cysts or microcysts; appears as a "smudgy" or amorphous distribution on CC but shows the characteristic **"tea-cup" or crescent** shape (layering) on the true lateral projection. Always obtain a 90° lateral magnification view if milk of calcium is suspected on CC — the diagnosis is made on the lateral, not the CC.

**Calcifications requiring judgement:**

**Grouped amorphous or punctate calcifications** (M3) are the most common diagnostic dilemma. The differential includes benign lobular calcifications (sclerosing adenosis, apocrine change) and low-grade DCIS. Targeted ultrasound should always be performed — a concordant cluster of microcysts confirms benignity. If ultrasound is negative or non-contributory, the choice between stereotactic biopsy and short-interval follow-up is a matter of local unit policy and individual case features. Many UK units proceed to biopsy given the DCIS overlap; this is defensible and reasonable.

**Fine pleomorphic, linear, or branching calcifications in a segmental or linear distribution** are suspicious (M4) or malignant (M5) until proven otherwise. Stereotactic biopsy is required. Magnification views define morphology and distribution; tomosynthesis helps map the ductal tree three-dimensionally.

The discipline of always considering the **three-dimensional ductal map** is important: segmental or linear distribution implies a single duct or ductal segment — suspicious. Diffuse, scattered, bilateral distribution implies systemic or hormonal process — almost always benign.

## Lymph Nodes

### Normal and Reactive Nodes

Intramammary and axillary lymph nodes are common incidental findings. The benign node has a **reniform shape, thin uniform cortex (<3 mm), and an echogenic fatty hilum with hilar vascularity** on ultrasound. On mammography the fatty hilum appears as a central lucency. Symmetry and multiplicity support benignity.

Reactive lymphadenopathy — uniform cortical thickening with preserved hilum and hilar hyperaemia — occurs with infection, recent vaccination (particularly axillary ipsilateral to recent COVID-19 vaccination), inflammatory conditions, and autoimmune disease. Clinical correlation is essential; a vaccination history should delay screening or explain reactive axillary adenopathy for up to 12 weeks.

Coarse calcifications in lymph nodes suggest granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) or tattoo pigment deposition — both benign.

**Suspicious nodal features warranting biopsy:** cortical thickness >3 mm (particularly if focal or eccentric), loss of echogenic hilum, rounded (rather than reniform) shape, peripheral/non-hilar vascularity, focal cortical mass. A single suspicious node in a woman with ipsilateral breast malignancy should be sampled — it changes staging and management.

## Skin and Subcutaneous Benign Lesions

Sebaceous cysts, epidermal inclusion cysts, dermatofibromas, and neurofibromas present as subdermal masses. Ultrasound shows a superficial, well-circumscribed, avascular lesion with variable internal echogenicity; clinical examination confirms the dermal origin. A skin marker and tangential view confirm mammographic correlation. These lesions do not require biopsy if clinically and sonographically characteristic.

## Infection and Inflammatory Conditions

**Lactational mastitis and abscess** are clinical diagnoses supported by imaging. Ultrasound demonstrates a hypoechoic, often loculated collection with an hyperaemic rim, internal debris, and non-compressibility. Ultrasound-guided aspiration or drain insertion, combined with antibiotics, is first-line management. Do not mistake a non-lactational breast abscess — particularly in a middle-aged or older woman — for simple infection without ensuring there is no underlying malignancy.

**Inflammatory carcinoma** — a clinical and pathological diagnosis — can mimic mastitis. The key distinguishing features are: non-lactational setting, absence of a drainable focal collection, diffuse skin thickening and oedema (peau d'orange), and a rapid progressive course. Any woman with apparent mastitis not resolving with antibiotics within two to four weeks must be urgently reassessed. Core biopsy of thickened skin or underlying tissue is required.

**Periareolar (Zuska's) abscesses** occur predominantly in smokers and are associated with squamous metaplasia of the terminal ducts. They tend to recur and can form fistulae. Management requires specialist breast surgery input alongside drainage.

**Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis** is a diagnosis of exclusion — clinically and radiologically it closely mimics malignancy, with irregular hypoechoic masses, sinus tracts, and abscess formation. Core biopsy is required both to confirm the diagnosis and to exclude carcinoma. Management is medical (steroids) or surgical per MDT, and recurrence is common.

## Special Situations

### Galactocoele

Milk retention in a lactating or recently lactating breast produces a complex cystic mass with variable fat-fluid levels depending on the fat-to-protein ratio of the retained milk. Aspiration yields milky fluid and is diagnostic. Management is conservative.

### Diabetic Mastopathy (Lymphocytic Mastitis)

Hard, palpable, often bilateral masses in women with long-standing type 1 diabetes or autoimmune disease. Ultrasound shows marked posterior acoustic shadowing from a hypoechoic, ill-defined area — an appearance that closely mimics invasive carcinoma. Core biopsy (B2 — lymphocytic lobulitis and stromal fibrosis) provides the diagnosis. Avoid repeated surgical excision; imaging–pathology concordance and MDT discussion prevent unnecessary operations. Recurrence after biopsy or excision is typical.

### Male Breast: Gynaecomastia

Gynaecomastia — the commonest male breast "abnormality" — has three classic patterns: **nodular** (subareolar mass), **dendritic** (flame-shaped retroareolar fibrosis extending into the breast), and **diffuse**. On mammography, the hallmark is a subareolar fan-shaped or triangular density tapering posteriorly. On ultrasound, subareolar hypoechoic tissue radiating posteriorly from the nipple.

Male breast carcinoma is rare but must not be missed. **A discrete, eccentric, or peripheral mass — particularly with associated calcifications — is not gynaecomastia and requires biopsy.** The distinction between the subareolar symmetry of gynaecomastia and the eccentric, often posterior or peripheral location of carcinoma is the critical discriminating feature.

## Non-Mass Asymmetries

Focal and global asymmetries are common findings in breast imaging. The key question is always: does this represent a real three-dimensional finding, or is it a summation artefact?

Features favouring benign summation or normal asymmetry:
- Disperses on spot compression or rolled views
- Absent or inconsistent on the orthogonal view
- Stable over several years of prior imaging
- Mirrors contralateral density distribution
- No associated architectural distortion, skin change, or suspicious calcifications
- Negative targeted ultrasound

Features that should prompt concern even in an apparent asymmetry:
- New or developing density
- Associated architectural distortion
- Persists on tomosynthesis
- Palpable correlate (P3+)
- Suspicious calcifications within the asymmetry

Management: M2 if classic summation with negative ultrasound and concordant tomosynthesis. M3 if persistent; perform targeted ultrasound and consider short-interval follow-up or biopsy depending on confidence, age, and prior history.

## Correlation Across Modalities and with Pathology

Think of multimodality assessment as a logical sequence, not a reflex:

1. **Define the mammographic problem** using standard descriptors (shape, margin, density for masses; morphology and distribution for calcifications). Assign an M score.
2. **Perform targeted ultrasound** for any focal mammographic finding — including calcifications (to look for a solid correlate or microcysts). Assign a U score.
3. **If ultrasound is concordantly benign** (e.g., simple cyst explaining a circumscribed mass), upgrade confidence to M2/U2 and discharge. Ultrasound resolves a substantial proportion of recalled mammographic findings.
4. **If ultrasound is negative but mammography is suspicious** (e.g., segmental fine pleomorphic calcifications), proceed to stereotactic biopsy. A negative ultrasound does not exclude DCIS.
5. **Add MRI** selectively: scar versus recurrence, occult primary with nodal metastases, implant complications, preoperative staging in selected cases. MRI enhancement kinetics are adjunctive — morphology and clinical context remain primary.
6. **Biopsy using the best modality for the target:** ultrasound guidance for masses; stereotactic (mammographic) guidance for calcifications without ultrasound correlate; tomosynthesis guidance for distortions visible only on tomosynthesis; MRI guidance for MRI-only lesions.
7. **Assess radiology–pathology concordance at MDT.** This is not a formality — discordance should trigger repeat biopsy or escalation. B3 outcomes (radial scar, ADH, lobular neoplasia, atypical papilloma) require MDT discussion; management (vacuum-assisted excision versus surveillance) varies between lesion type and unit policy.

## When to Biopsy a "Probably Benign" Finding

The UK system does not require biopsy of every indeterminate finding, but it does require clear justification for not biopsying. Apply the following reasoning:

**Do not biopsy if:**
- Classic simple cyst (U2)
- Classic fibroadenoma with typical imaging in a woman under 40 (M2/U2)
- Classic hamartoma with fat component (M2/U2)
- Oil cyst with eggshell rim calcification (M2)
- Pathognomonic benign calcifications: milk of calcium, dermal, vascular, popcorn (M2)
- Typical IMLN with preserved fatty hilum (M2/U2)

**Biopsy if:**
- New or enlarging circumscribed mass in a woman over 40
- Grouped amorphous calcifications that cannot be confidently attributed to a benign cause on ultrasound
- Architectural distortion without a clear benign explanation (e.g., no scar, no prior trauma)
- Complex cystic-solid mass with any vascular solid component
- Clinical findings discordant with imaging (P3+ with M2/U2 — do not be falsely reassured)
- Any imaging–pathology discordance following initial biopsy

Acknowledge that practice varies. Some units have lower thresholds for biopsy (particularly for grouped amorphous calcifications or B3 lesions); others use imaging surveillance for selected cases. Know your unit's policy and the reasoning behind it.

## A Systematic Approach to Reporting Benign Findings

Clear, precise reporting language matters. When reporting a benign finding, include:

1. **The finding and its characteristics** — location, size, shape, margin, density/echogenicity, vascularity
2. **The modalities used** and the M/U scores assigned
3. **The most likely diagnosis** and why
4. **Any differentials** considered and why they have been excluded
5. **A clear management recommendation** — discharge, surveillance, biopsy — with justification
6. **Comparison with prior imaging** and the conclusion drawn from stability or change

Avoid vague language such as "probably benign — suggest clinical review." Make a recommendation. If you are uncertain, say so explicitly and escalate to MDT.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Understand the anatomy and histology** behind each entity — pattern recognition built on principles lasts; isolated facts do not.
- **Margins, internal architecture, and distribution** are more discriminating than size alone.
- **Tomosynthesis and targeted ultrasound** resolve the majority of benign mammographic mimics; use them systematically.
- **Prior imaging is your most powerful tool** — stability over years is strong evidence of benignity; interval change demands explanation.
- **Apply the UK five-point system consistently** and use it to structure your reports and recommendations.
- **Radiology–pathology concordance is not optional** — discordance must be resolved at MDT.
- **Avoid dogmatism:** some reassuring-looking lesions need biopsy; some worrying patterns are benign. When uncertain, document your reasoning, correlate across modalities, and discuss at MDT.

Mastering normal variants and benign appearances — and the reasoning behind them — is how you protect screening performance, reduce unnecessary intervention, and still find the cancers that hide among everyday findings.
