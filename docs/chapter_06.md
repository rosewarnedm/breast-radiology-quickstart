# Chapter 6: Suspicious Mammographic Features

## Overview and purpose

Recognising suspicious mammographic features is not about memorising lists — it is about understanding what breast cancer *does* to tissue and how those changes translate into visible patterns on film. Malignant cells invade and provoke a desmoplastic stromal reaction; ducts fill with necrotic debris that calcifies; infiltrating tumour tethers Cooper's ligaments and distorts the normal radial architecture. Every suspicious mammographic sign flows from one of these processes. If you understand the biology, the appearances become predictable.

This chapter works through the major suspicious categories, explains the differential diagnosis for each, and shows how to synthesise mammography with tomosynthesis, ultrasound, and biopsy within the NHSBSP and symptomatic triple assessment pathways. Scoring (the M-scale), biopsy selection, and radiology–pathology concordance are threaded throughout.

## The UK context: where mammography sits in the pathway

### Screening: NHSBSP

Eligible women aged 50–70 are invited every three years for two-view mammography (craniocaudal, CC, and mediolateral oblique, MLO). Some programmes extend this to 47–73 as part of the age extension trial. All screening mammograms are double-read; discordance triggers arbitration. Recalls attend a dedicated assessment clinic where additional views, tomosynthesis, targeted ultrasound, and image-guided biopsy are performed as indicated. Prior imaging is reviewed whenever available — comparison is not optional.

### Symptomatic pathway: triple assessment

Triple assessment integrates:
- **Clinical examination**, scored P1–P5
- **Imaging**, scored M1–M5 (mammogram) and U1–U5 (ultrasound)
- **Histology**, scored B1–B5

The imaging score reflects the probability of malignancy and directs action:

| Score | Meaning | Action |
|-------|---------|--------|
| M1 | Normal | Routine |
| M2 | Benign | Reassurance |
| M3 | Indeterminate/probably benign | MDT discussion; in practice often leads to biopsy |
| M4 | Suspicious | Biopsy |
| M5 | Highly suggestive of malignancy | Biopsy |

In UK symptomatic practice, M3 is not a comfortable resting place — most indeterminate findings in an assessment clinic will proceed to biopsy rather than short-interval follow-up, unless there is a confident benign explanation and MDT agreement. This distinguishes UK practice from some international approaches.

### Modality roles

- **Mammography**: the primary tool for detecting and characterising calcifications, masses, distortion, and asymmetries.
- **Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT)**: greatly improves conspicuity of distortion and spiculation by removing overlapping tissue; widely used in UK assessment clinics.
- **Ultrasound**: characterises masses, evaluates asymmetries, assesses axillary nodes, and guides core biopsy of sonographically visible targets.
- **MRI**: reserved for problem-solving and locoregional staging in selected cases; not a first-line screening tool in the general population.
- **Image-guided biopsy**: ultrasound-guided core biopsy for visible masses; stereotactic or DBT-guided vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB) for calcifications and distortion without a sonographic correlate.

## Anatomy that drives appearance

Understanding normal anatomy makes the abnormal self-evident.

The breast is a branching duct–lobular tree embedded in fibrous stroma and fat. Ducts converge radially to the nipple; Cooper's ligaments tether skin to deep fascia. On mammography, fat is radiolucent (dark) and fibroglandular tissue is radiodense (pale). Cancer invades the stroma, triggering a desmoplastic reaction that pulls on Cooper's ligaments — producing spiculation and skin or nipple retraction.

Calcifications arise in different contexts:
- **Within ducts**: necrosis inside DCIS-involved ducts produces fine, irregular, angular calcification — the most suspicious pattern.
- **Dystrophic**: following trauma, surgery, or fat necrosis — typically coarse and irregular but large.
- **Secretory/involutional**: in lobules and duct walls — usually coarse, bilateral, and benign.

Architectural distortion occurs whenever the normal radial-lamellar arrangement is pulled or scarred — by malignant infiltration, a radial scar, or prior surgery.

Oedema and trabecular thickening reflect lymphatic obstruction, occurring in inflammatory carcinoma, extensive nodal involvement, and — importantly — benign causes such as cardiac failure. Clinical context is essential.

## How to look: perception, analysis, and avoiding misses

Adopt a systematic approach every time. On both views, sweep through:

1. **Skin and nipple** — thickening, retraction, asymmetry
2. **Subareolar region** — ductal change, asymmetric density
3. **Retroareolar and central breast**
4. **Quadrants** — comparing left and right side-by-side
5. **Posterior tissue** — a common blind spot; ensure pectoral muscle reaches the nipple level on MLO
6. **Axillary tail** — intramammary and axillary nodes
7. **Medial breast on CC** — frequently under-scrutinised

**Triangulate location** by comparing the CC and MLO views to localise a lesion in quadrant and depth (anterior, middle, posterior third). This directly informs where to direct the ultrasound probe.

**Use optimised additional views**:
- *Spot compression*: resolves summation artefact and better defines margins — an essential step before upgrading or dismissing an asymmetry.
- *Magnification CC and 90-degree lateral*: refine microcalcification morphology and reveal layering (milk of calcium settles dependently on the true lateral).
- *DBT*: the single most useful tool for distortion and equivocal masses in assessment.

**Avoid technical pitfalls**:
- Motion blur softens margins and calcifications — repeat if present; do not accept a blurred study.
- Underexposed or overexposed images hide low-contrast findings — check technical quality before reporting.
- Poor positioning misses posterior and medial lesions — if in doubt, request additional views.
- Skin lesions (naevi, papillomas) project as apparent parenchymal calcifications — mark with a skin BB marker to confirm dermal location.

## Suspicious mammographic categories

### 1) Masses

A mass is a space-occupying lesion visible on *two projections*. A density seen on one view only is an asymmetry until proven otherwise — do not call it a mass.

Analyse each mass by **shape**, **margins**, **density**, and **associated features**.

#### Shape

- **Irregular or angular**: suggests invasion — raise suspicion.
- **Round or oval**: favours benignity (cyst, fibroadenoma) but is not definitive. Some cancers — mucinous carcinoma, triple-negative, haematogenous metastases — are well-circumscribed. Do not rely on shape alone.

#### Margins

Margins are the most diagnostically powerful feature.

- **Spiculated**: thin straight lines radiate from the mass margin, produced by the desmoplastic reaction tethering Cooper's ligaments. Spiculation is highly predictive of malignancy and almost always warrants M5, unless a clear benign explanation exists at exactly that site (e.g. a known recent surgical scar in precisely the same location).
- **Indistinct/obscured**: raises concern, especially if persistent on spot compression and DBT — pursue further.
- **Microlobulated**: a fine, undulating contour with multiple small lobulations can indicate an invasive tumour front; suspicious when numerous and fine.
- **Circumscribed**: usually benign, but cannot be assumed safe without ultrasound correlation. A round, well-circumscribed lesion that is anechoic on ultrasound is a simple cyst and needs nothing further; if solid, proceed to characterisation and often biopsy.

#### Density

A mass *denser than the adjacent fibroglandular tissue* is more suspicious. Fat-containing masses (oil cysts, hamartomas, lipomas) are almost always benign — the visible fat is the key.

#### Associated features

Any of the following *increase* the M-score:
- Architectural distortion radiating from the mass
- Skin thickening or retraction
- Nipple retraction
- Suspicious associated calcifications
- Oedema or trabecular thickening
- Abnormal ipsilateral nodes

#### Key differentials

**Fibroadenoma vs carcinoma**: Fibroadenomas are oval, circumscribed, homogeneous, and often show gentle macrolobulation. As they involute, they develop coarse "popcorn" calcification — a benign pattern you should recognise confidently. On ultrasound they are parallel, homogeneous, and wider than tall. Carcinomas typically show irregular shape, non-circumscribed margins, higher relative density, and architectural distortion.

**Fat necrosis vs carcinoma**: Fat necrosis is a great mimic. Look for internal fat lucency within the lesion, an oil cyst, or evolving rim calcification — these features support benignity. However, spiculated distortion without any fat component and no relevant surgical history should not be attributed to fat necrosis without biopsy.

**Intramammary lymph node**: Oval, circumscribed, typically upper outer quadrant, with a visible radiolucent fatty hilum. Suspicious features — loss of hilum, round shape, eccentric cortical bulge — warrant ultrasound and potentially biopsy.

#### Scoring

- Spiculated mass: M5.
- Irregular, high-density, non-circumscribed mass with distortion: M5.
- New or enlarging ill-defined mass without classic malignant morphology: M4; proceed to DBT and ultrasound before or at biopsy.

### 2) Microcalcifications

Calcification morphology and distribution are the keys to identifying DCIS and some invasive cancers. Train your eye to distinguish *what the crystals look like* and *where they are distributed*. These two axes together determine the score.

#### Morphology

**Suspicious patterns** (learn these first):

- *Fine linear or fine linear branching ("casting") calcifications*: Calcified necrotic debris filling and casting the shape of involved ducts. Irregular, discontinuous, sometimes branching. This is the hallmark of high-grade DCIS — **M5**.
- *Fine pleomorphic*: Variable in shape and size, typically tiny and angular, lacking the elongated branching pattern of casting calcifications. **M4–M5** depending on distribution.
- *Amorphous/indistinct*: Small, hazy deposits without clear morphological detail. Can represent low-grade DCIS or benign secretory change. Context and distribution matter; a tight new cluster is often **M3–M4**.
- *Coarse heterogeneous*: Larger than pleomorphic, variable density; intermediate suspicion when clustered.

**Benign patterns** (be confident recognising these to avoid unnecessary recall):

- *Skin calcifications*: Lucent-centred, round or oval; confirm with a BB marker that they project over skin.
- *Vascular calcifications*: Parallel "tram-track" pattern following arterial walls.
- *Milk of calcium*: Layering cystic calcifications; on the CC view they appear smudged or ill-defined, but on the 90-degree true lateral they settle into a crescentic or "tea-cup" appearance — this characteristic change between views is diagnostic and reassuring.
- *Dystrophic calcifications*: Large, coarse, irregular, typically at a known surgical or traumatic site.
- *Secretory/"plasma cell" calcifications*: Rod-like, often bilateral, radiating towards the nipple; usually benign.

#### Distribution

Distribution is as important as morphology — a coarse heterogeneous cluster in a tight focal area is more worrying than a few similar calcifications scattered diffusely across both breasts.

- *Segmental or linear*: Following the trajectory of a duct system towards the nipple — **strongly suspicious** for DCIS; score M4–M5 depending on morphology.
- *Grouped/clustered*: Tight focal cluster — suspicious if morphology is concerning; extent and morphology together drive the score.
- *Regional*: Scattered in a large volume — worrying only if morphology is clearly suspicious; diffuse bilateral calcifications are nearly always benign.

#### Work-up

Always obtain **magnification CC and 90-degree lateral** views for any calcification cluster you are uncertain about. These improve morphological detail and reveal layering. DBT is less helpful than magnification 2D for characterising fine calcification morphology.

#### Biopsy and concordance

Stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB is the standard for calcification sampling. Obtain a specimen radiograph to confirm calcifications are present in the cores. Place a marker clip. If the biopsy returns B2 for an M4 or M5 cluster, this is a **discordant result** — repeat biopsy or surgical excision is required; do not accept benign histology for a clearly suspicious target.

Common pathological correlates:
- Fine linear/branching → high-grade DCIS
- Fine pleomorphic → intermediate- or high-grade DCIS
- Amorphous → low-grade DCIS, sclerosing adenosis, or benign secretory change

### 3) Architectural distortion (AD)

**Definition**: Focal disruption of the normal parenchymal architecture with radiating lines from a point and no discrete mass visible.

AD is easy to miss on 2D mammography, particularly in dense breasts where it blends into overlapping tissue. **DBT is transformative for detecting and analysing AD** — it is a major reason why tomosynthesis has become standard in UK assessment clinics.

#### Differential diagnosis

The two most important causes are **invasive carcinoma** and **radial scar/complex sclerosing lesion (CSL)**. Crucially, these can look *identical* on mammography. You cannot reliably distinguish them without biopsy.

Other differentials:
- *Post-surgical scar*: Tethering and fat lucency at a known operative site; tends to stabilise or regress over time — use priors. A distortion that is growing or developing new calcifications at a surgical site needs reassessment.
- *Fat necrosis*: Distortion with internal fat lucency or rim calcification supports benignity.
- *Focal fibrosis/sclerosing adenosis*: Indistinguishable from the above on imaging; requires biopsy.

#### Assessment and biopsy

Score isolated, new AD as **M4** as a minimum. Spiculated distortion with associated calcifications or a suspicious ultrasound correlate warrants **M5**.

- If no sonographic correlate: DBT-guided or stereotactic VAB.
- If sonographic correlate: ultrasound-guided core biopsy is appropriate and usually faster.

**Radial scar/CSL returning B3** is a common and important scenario. Management is evolving, but many UK centres proceed to vacuum-assisted excision with MDT discussion, given the upgrade rate to atypia or malignancy. Be familiar with your local MDT protocol.

### 4) Asymmetries

An asymmetry is unilateral increased tissue density on one or more views that does *not* meet the criteria for a discrete mass (no convex margins, no specific architecture). Work through a hierarchy based on how convincingly the asymmetry is established and whether it is new.

#### Types

- **One-view asymmetry**: Seen on one view only — most often summation artefact. Obtain spot compression and orthogonal views before making any decisions. If it persists on two views, manage as a focal asymmetry.
- **Focal asymmetry**: Seen on two views but without clear margins. If new or increasing, warrants DBT and targeted ultrasound. Can be scored M3–M4 depending on whether it persists after compression and whether ultrasound reveals a correlate.
- **Developing asymmetry**: New or enlarging compared with prior studies — this is the most significant subtype. A developing asymmetry that persists on DBT and has no benign explanation should be scored **M4** and biopsied.
- **Global asymmetry**: Usually a normal variant or post-surgical. Malignancy is uncommon unless accompanied by skin thickening, nipple change, or other suspicious features.

#### Correlation

Perform targeted ultrasound in the corresponding quadrant and depth. If no sonographic correlate is found but mammographic concern persists, consider DBT, MRI, or — where the index of suspicion is high — stereotactic biopsy rather than dismissal.

#### Pitfalls

Positioning differences between studies can create pseudo-asymmetry — ensure like-for-like comparison before calling a new finding. Hormonal change can increase global density. Check if the patient has started hormone replacement therapy since the last study.

### 5) Ductal and subareolar changes

A solitary dilated duct with internal material, calcifications, or an associated intraductal mass raises concern for papilloma or DCIS. The key suspicious scenario is:

- **Segmental fine calcifications extending towards the nipple** — high risk for DCIS; score M4–M5.
- **Nipple discharge** (especially spontaneous, single-duct, and blood-stained) in association with any subareolar abnormality — escalate urgently.

Ultrasound is often helpful in identifying intraductal masses. Galactography is rarely used in UK practice. Any persistent subareolar abnormality on mammography warrants ultrasound correlation and a low threshold for biopsy.

---

### 6) Skin, nipple, trabeculae, and oedema

- **Skin thickening**: Localised thickening tethered to a spiculated underlying mass is suspicious. Generalised thickening with trabecular prominence raises concern for inflammatory carcinoma but also occurs with benign oedema (cardiac failure, renal failure, mastitis). Clinical examination is essential — do not over-call on imaging alone.
- **Nipple retraction**: *New* retraction with subareolar distortion is suspicious (M4–M5). Long-standing stable bilateral inversion is usually normal.
- **Trabecular thickening and oedema**: Non-specific in isolation; significantly more suspicious when associated with a mass, distortion, or clinically inflamed breast.

---

### 7) Lymph nodes

Normal intramammary and axillary nodes are oval, circumscribed, and have a visible radiolucent fatty hilum with a thin cortex. Suspicious features include:

- Round shape or cortical thickening, particularly if eccentric
- Loss of the fatty hilum
- Microcalcifications within the node (can reflect metastatic DCIS or — confusingly — silicone deposits)

Correlate with ultrasound, which allows measurement of cortical thickness and assessment of hilar flow. Biopsy abnormal nodes in the appropriate clinical context; this can upstage disease significantly and alter surgical planning.

## Distinguishing benign from suspicious: underlying principles

Rather than applying rigid rules, internalise the following principles:

**1. Patterns that respect normal anatomy tend to be benign; patterns that defy it raise suspicion.**
Multiple similar well-circumscribed masses, diffuse bilateral calcifications, or global symmetrical density are most often variants of normal. Spicules pulling tissue from multiple directions, linear calcifications marching in a ductal path towards the nipple, or asymmetric regional distortion should not be dismissed.

**2. Interval change is the most powerful contextual factor.**
New or enlarging findings demand explanation. A developing asymmetry or a new cluster of amorphous calcifications requires action even if the morphology alone would be borderline. Conversely, documented stability over many years reduces — though does not eliminate — concern. Be cautious about stability in low-grade DCIS, which can smoulder quietly.

**3. Density and margin trump size.**
A 5 mm spiculated mass is far more worrying than a 2 cm well-circumscribed, fat-containing lesion. Do not let a small lesion falsely reassure you.

**4. Associated features elevate the score.**
A mass that you might otherwise score M3 becomes M4 or M5 if accompanied by skin thickening, nipple retraction, oedema, or an abnormal node.

## Modality correlation and problem-solving

### Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT)

DBT acquires multiple low-dose projections across an arc and reconstructs thin slices, removing the obscuring effect of overlapping tissue. It is particularly valuable for:

- Clarifying margins and spiculation of masses
- Detecting and characterising architectural distortion
- Resolving one-view asymmetries (most resolve; those that persist are more concerning)
- Guiding biopsy of subtle distortion

Its limitation is calcification morphology — fine calcifications can be smeared across reconstructed slices. Always use **magnification 2D** for calcification characterisation; do not rely solely on DBT for this.

### Ultrasound

Ultrasound is your most important secondary tool for masses and asymmetries. Use mammographic coordinates — quadrant, depth from nipple, clock position — to direct the probe. The key suspicious ultrasound features are:

- Irregular or angular margins
- Non-parallel (taller-than-wide) orientation
- Posterior acoustic shadowing
- Echogenic halo
- Heterogeneous echotexture

**If there is no sonographic correlate for a clearly suspicious mammographic target, this is not reassuring — proceed to stereotactic or DBT-guided biopsy rather than downgrading the score.** The absence of an ultrasound finding does not exclude malignancy, particularly for distortion and DCIS.

### MRI

Reserve MRI for specific indications: discordant or equivocal findings after full work-up, staging of invasive lobular carcinoma (whose extent is often underestimated on mammography), occult primary with nodal metastasis, and problem-solving when other modalities are inconclusive. Be aware that low-grade DCIS may show no enhancement on MRI, so a negative MRI does not exclude it.

## Reporting and scoring in UK practice

A good mammography report describes, interprets, scores, and acts. It should not be merely descriptive.

**Describe** the dominant abnormality using precise terms: shape, margins, density, size, and location for masses; morphology, distribution, and extent for calcifications; focality and associated features for distortion and asymmetries.

**Assign an M-score** that matches your narrative and reflects your genuine assessment of malignancy likelihood.

**Recommend the next step within the same report**: targeted ultrasound, magnification views, DBT, or a specific biopsy route. In a screening assessment clinic, clearly state whether the original screening abnormality has been explained — do not discharge until the screening prompt is resolved.

**Document interval change explicitly**: "New compared with prior study dated..." or "Stable over X years" is clinically useful; vague language is not.

## Image-guided biopsy: choosing the right approach and ensuring concordance

### Choosing the biopsy route

- **Ultrasound-guided core biopsy**: first-line for any sonographically visible mass or asymmetry. Fast, real-time, and well-tolerated. Place a marker clip if future localisation may be needed.
- **Stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB**: preferred for calcifications and architectural distortion without a sonographic correlate. Obtain a specimen radiograph to confirm calcification retrieval. Always place a clip.
- **DBT-guided biopsy**: increasingly used for subtle distortion identified only on tomosynthesis slices.

### The B-score system

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| B1 | Non-diagnostic (inadequate sample) |
| B2 | Benign and concordant |
| B3 | Lesion of uncertain malignant potential (e.g. radial scar, ADH, flat epithelial atypia, papillary lesion) |
| B4 | Suspicious but not diagnostic |
| B5a | Malignant — in situ |
| B5b | Malignant — invasive |

### Concordance — the critical step

Radiology–pathology concordance must be formally assessed for every biopsy result:

- **B2 result for an M2 target**: concordant — reassure and discharge or arrange appropriate follow-up.
- **B2 result for an M4 or M5 target**: **discordant** — this requires repeat biopsy or surgical excision. A benign result does not cancel a suspicious imaging finding.
- **B3 result**: Requires MDT discussion. Many UK centres manage radial scar and ADH with vacuum-assisted excision given upgrade rates to malignancy of around 10–30% depending on the entity and degree of atypia.
- **B4 result**: Treat as suspicious; surgical excision is generally indicated.

Clip migration can occur between biopsy and any subsequent procedure — confirm clip position on post-procedure imaging, particularly for calcifications where you should also document the extent of remaining calcification.

## Common benign entities that mimic malignancy

Know these well — they are the most frequent causes of unnecessary biopsy or, more dangerously, of mistaking a false reassuring appearance for benignity.

- **Radial scar/CSL**: Star-like distortion with a central lucency and long, thin spicules on magnification — the "black star." Cannot be reliably distinguished from malignancy on imaging; biopsy is mandatory.
- **Sclerosing adenosis**: Clustered amorphous calcifications or distortion; biopsy required.
- **Fat necrosis**: Oil cysts, rim calcifications, and distortion — all potentially alarming. Look for the internal fat lucency that confirms the benign nature.
- **Post-surgical scar**: Tethered distortion at the operative site; stabilises or shrinks over time. Any enlarging distortion at a known scar site needs reassessment, not dismissal.
- **Fibroadenoma with involutional calcification**: Coarse "popcorn" calcifications are reassuringly benign; atypical calcification patterns within a fibroadenoma may require biopsy.
- **Secretory/plasma cell mastitis calcifications**: Coarse, bilateral, rod-like, periductal — usually readily recognised as benign.
- **PASH and focal fibrosis**: May present as a palpable mass or asymmetry; core biopsy resolves uncertainty.

## A practical approach to a suspicious finding

Use this mental framework for every assessment:

1. **Check priors first.** Is this new, growing, or changing? New or developing findings demand explanation.

2. **Describe the dominant abnormality precisely**:
   - *Mass*: shape, margins, density, size, location, associated features.
   - *Calcifications*: morphology, distribution, extent.
   - *Distortion*: focality, associated calcifications, visibility on DBT.
   - *Asymmetry*: one-view vs. focal vs. developing; does it persist on spot compression and DBT?

3. **Correlate with targeted ultrasound**:
   - Does a plausible benign correlate (e.g. simple cyst, typical node) explain the finding?
   - Is there a suspicious correlate (irregular hypoechoic mass, non-parallel lesion) that upgrades or confirms the concern?
   - No sonographic correlate for a suspicious mammographic target → proceed to stereotactic/DBT biopsy, not downgrading.

4. **Assign an M-score** consistent with your narrative and planned action.

5. **Close the loop**: ensure biopsy results are reviewed for concordance, with MDT discussion for B3 and discordant results.

## Common misses and how to avoid them

- **Subtle spiculation hidden by overlapping tissue**: use DBT liberally in assessment — do not accept a 2D mammogram alone for a possible distortion.
- **Faint amorphous calcification clusters**: scrutinise areas of density with magnification views; compare systematically with priors for any new cluster, however subtle.
- **Posterior or medial lesions**: always check positioning quality; request lateromedial or cleavage views if needed.
- **Subareolar lesions**: use careful compression magnification views and ultrasound; do not dismiss new nipple retraction without subareolar imaging.
- **Circumscribed mass assumed benign without correlation**: every solid circumscribed mass requires ultrasound; some malignancies are well-circumscribed.

## Summary

Suspicious mammographic features make sense when you understand the pathology behind them. Desmoplasia produces spiculation; ductal necrosis produces fine linear calcification; infiltrative growth disrupts normal architecture; lymphatic obstruction causes skin and trabecular change. In UK practice, two-view mammography with double reading, structured assessment using DBT and targeted ultrasound, and image-guided biopsy within triple assessment constitute a robust diagnostic pathway.

Your role as a reporter is to match morphology and distribution to the underlying process, actively identify and explain interval change, assign an M-score that genuinely reflects malignancy probability and drives appropriate action, and ensure formal radiology–pathology concordance. Use the scoring system as a structured guide, not a formula — context, priors, and clinical information always matter. When findings are borderline or discordant, escalate through biopsy and MDT discussion rather than relying on a single imaging modality to provide false certainty.
