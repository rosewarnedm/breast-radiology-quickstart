# Chapter 4: A Systematic Interpretation Approach

---

## Why a system matters

Breast imaging combines high volume with high stakes. A single screening session may involve reading hundreds of mammograms, each carrying a small but real probability of harbouring a cancer that looks very much like normal tissue. Two cognitive hazards dominate: *missing* a subtle abnormality because it blends into background density, and *over-calling* a normal variant and subjecting a woman to unnecessary biopsy. A disciplined, repeatable method protects against both.

The specific risks in breast radiology are worth naming explicitly:

- **Satisfaction of search** — finding one lesion and unconsciously switching off, missing a second.
- **Anchoring** — forming a premature diagnosis and selectively attending to supporting features.
- **Availability bias** — over-weighting diagnoses you have seen recently.
- **Confirmation bias** — interpreting ultrasound to match a mammographic preconception.

No system eliminates these biases, but a structured approach makes them more visible. The aim is not to follow a rigid protocol mechanically but to build a mental scaffold that you apply consistently and can defend at the multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting.

## The UK context

Before touching a study, orient yourself to the pathway the patient is on — this shapes what you are looking for and what is expected of your report.

**NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP):** Women aged 50–70 are invited for two-view mammography (cranio-caudal [CC] and medio-lateral oblique [MLO]) every three years. Women over 70 may self-refer. All screening films are double-read; discordances are resolved at arbitration. The programme operates under explicit performance standards balancing cancer detection against recall rate — recall is not free, and an unnecessary recall causes real anxiety and consumes assessment clinic resources.

**Triple assessment** is the backbone of both symptomatic and recalled screening assessment. It integrates clinical, radiological, and pathological information into a coherent decision:

| Score | Modality | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Clinical/palpation | P1 normal → P5 malignant |
| **M** | Mammography | M1 normal → M5 malignant |
| **U** | Ultrasound | U1 normal → U5 malignant |
| **B** | Core biopsy histology | B1 non-diagnostic → B5b invasive |

The power of triple assessment lies in *concordance*: when P, M, U, and B all point in the same direction, you can act with confidence. Discordance is a signal to pause and reconsider, not to ignore.

**Modality roles in UK practice:**

- **Mammography (2D digital ± DBT):** The screening backbone. Best for calcifications and architectural distortion.
- **Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT):** Reduces tissue overlap, improving mass margin characterisation and distortion detection. Widely used in assessment; increasingly used in primary screening in some centres.
- **Ultrasound:** First-line for focal symptoms and for characterising or targeting masses and cysts found on mammography.
- **MRI:** Problem-solving, extent-of-disease mapping, implant assessment, and high-risk screening within defined pathways.
- **Image-guided biopsy:** Ultrasound-guided wherever possible; stereotactic or tomosynthesis-guided for calcifications and distortion; MRI-guided for MRI-only lesions.

## A four-pass approach

Think of your read as four deliberate passes, each with a specific purpose:

1. **Preparation and technical adequacy**
2. **Global overview and context**
3. **Structured search and lesion analysis**
4. **Correlation, concordance, and management**

The temptation — especially under time pressure — is to skip straight to pass 3. Resist it. Passes 1 and 2 provide the scaffolding within which pass 3 findings make sense, and pass 4 is where errors of reasoning most often occur.

## Pass 1: Preparation and technical adequacy

### Clinical context and prior images

Confirm the indication before looking at the images. Is this a screening study or a symptomatic referral? If symptomatic, what is the precise complaint — lump, pain, nipple discharge, skin change — and where exactly? What is the patient's risk profile (family history, prior cancer, BRCA status, prior radiotherapy)? Is she pregnant, lactating, on hormone replacement therapy (HRT)?

**Always retrieve prior studies.** Prior images frequently convert an uncertain finding into a confident one. A lesion that has been stable for six years is almost certainly benign; one that has grown in 18 months needs biopsy. Do not report without priors if they are available — this is a patient safety issue.

### Mammographic positioning and quality

Inadequate positioning is one of the most common causes of missed cancers. Assess both views critically before interpreting them.

**MLO view:**
- Pectoral muscle should reach the level of the posterior nipple line (PNL) or below, with a convex anterior border (confirming adequate muscle relaxation).
- Inframammary fold should be open and included.
- Adequate posterior tissue; nipple in profile where possible; no significant skin folds.

**CC view:**
- PNL on CC should be within 1 cm of PNL on MLO (a greater discrepancy suggests posterior tissue has been excluded).
- Medial tissue fully included; no motion blur.

**Compression:** Adequate compression reduces motion, separates summating tissues, and improves image contrast. Lesions hidden by overlap on poorly compressed views may become visible with good compression — or may disappear, confirming they were summation artefacts.

If positioning is genuinely suboptimal and the area of concern may be excluded, flag this in your report and consider requesting repeat views before making a management decision.

### DBT-specific quality checks

DBT reduces tissue overlap but introduces its own artefacts — step artefact at the skin surface, motion streaks, and occasional mimics of distortion from noise. Track any suspicious finding through multiple consecutive slices; a real lesion persists, whilst an artefact does not. When synthetic 2D images are provided alongside DBT, use them for calcification assessment (2D magnification remains superior for calcification morphology) and confirm key findings on DBT slices.

### Ultrasound technical adequacy

Use a high-frequency linear transducer (typically 12–18 MHz). Optimise depth so the lesion of interest is central in the field, focus at or just below the lesion, and apply *minimal* pressure — excess pressure compresses posterior acoustic features and may collapse superficial cysts. Adjust time-gain compensation to equalise background echogenicity. Document each lesion with clockface position, distance from nipple, size in three dimensions, and relationship to skin and chest wall.

### MRI technical adequacy

Check that the correct protocol has been performed: high-resolution T1 pre-contrast, dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) series with adequate temporal resolution for kinetic assessment, fat-suppressed subtractions, MIP reconstruction, T2/STIR, and silicone-sensitive sequences if implants are present. Timing relative to the menstrual cycle matters — ideally days 7–14 to minimise background parenchymal enhancement (BPE). Note motion artefacts and fat–water swap, which can degrade or obscure findings.

Before analysing individual lesions, take a step back. The goal of this pass is to orientate yourself to the overall picture and set your expectations.

On mammography, assess:
- **Symmetry** — are the two breasts broadly similar in density and tissue distribution?
- **Breast density** — density reduces mammographic sensitivity and increases the value of DBT and adjunct ultrasound. Your unit may formally categorise density (A–D); even if not, be aware of its impact on your confidence.
- **Skin and nipple** — thickening, oedema, retraction, or asymmetric flattening.
- **Trabecular pattern** — diffuse trabecular thickening may indicate lymphoedema, inflammatory carcinoma, or cardiac failure; focal thickening is more suspicious.
- **Obvious masses, distortions, or calcification clusters** — note them but do not stop here; complete the global pass before analysing.
- **Axillary lymph nodes** — scan quickly for obvious adenopathy.
- **Chest and lung** visible on MLO — incidental findings do occur.

On ultrasound, mentally map the quadrants and subareolar region before zooming in.

On MRI, review the MIP to identify all enhancing foci before scrutinising individual lesions.

This global pass also activates your contextual knowledge: very dense breasts should prompt careful attention to DBT and correlation with ultrasound; prior surgery in the upper outer quadrant means you should expect postoperative distortion and fat necrosis in that region.

Use a consistent anatomical search route so that no region is systematically overlooked. On mammography and DBT, one practical sequence is:

1. Skin and nipple–areolar complex
2. Subcutaneous fat
3. Fibroglandular tissue, central to peripheral
4. Retroareolar region and ducts
5. Retromammary space, pectoral edge, chest wall
6. Axillary tail and visible axilla

Mirror this on the contralateral breast before moving on. On ultrasound, scan radially around the nipple, then sweep orthogonally, always returning to the symptomatic area or mammographic target. On MRI, review T2 first for cysts and oedema, then DCE subtractions and MIPs, then kinetics and internal enhancement patterns.

As you search, classify what you find into one of four mammographic lesion types. This classification drives your subsequent analysis.

---

### Masses

**Mammographic descriptors** (use these precisely in your reports):

- *Shape:* round, oval, or lobulated (more often benign) versus irregular (suspicious).
- *Margins:* circumscribed (well-defined), indistinct, microlobulated, obscured (hidden by overlying tissue — not necessarily suspicious), angular, or spiculated (most suspicious, reflecting infiltrative desmoplastic growth).
- *Density:* fat-density masses (lipoma, oil cyst, hamartoma) are reassuring. High-density masses compared with glandular tissue raise suspicion.
- *Associated features:* radiating spicules, skin or nipple retraction, trabecular thickening, satellite calcifications, ductal changes.

**Ultrasound correlation** — always characterise solid masses on ultrasound. Key descriptors:

- *Echo pattern:* anechoic (simple cyst), complex cystic and solid, hypoechoic solid, heterogeneous.
- *Shape and margins:* circumscribed versus angular, microlobulated.
- *Orientation:* wider-than-tall (parallel, more often benign) versus taller-than-wide (non-parallel, more suspicious).
- *Posterior features:* acoustic enhancement (typical of cysts; also seen in some high-grade cancers) or acoustic shadowing (desmoplastic reaction, or occasionally benign sclerosing adenosis).
- *Vascularity:* internal Doppler flow confirms solid nature and supports higher grade; absence does not exclude malignancy.

**Understanding the underlying biology helps you reason rather than pattern-match:**

- Spiculation reflects desmoplastic stromal reaction and infiltrative tumour growth — the most specific mammographic sign of invasive carcinoma.
- A smoothly circumscribed oval mass in a woman in her twenties or thirties is statistically likely to be a fibroadenoma — but the statistics only help you when the clinical context is concordant.
- High-grade (often triple-negative) carcinomas can be deceptively round and circumscribed, mimicking fibroadenomas. If a "circumscribed" mass has interval growth, internal heterogeneity, or any clinical suspicion, biopsy rather than reassure.

**Common differentials and discriminating features:**

| Diagnosis | Key features |
|---|---|
| Simple cyst | Anechoic, imperceptible wall, posterior enhancement, compressible |
| Complicated/proteinaceous cyst | Mobile layering echoes, no Doppler flow within "solid" content |
| Fibroadenoma | Oval, circumscribed, homogeneously hypoechoic, parallel, gentle lobulation |
| Phyllodes tumour | Rapid growth, large size (>3–4 cm), lobulated, heterogeneous with cystic clefts; distinguish histologically |
| Intramammary lymph node | Fatty hilum on mammography; oval with thin cortex and echogenic fatty hilum on US, upper outer quadrant or axillary tail |
| Fat necrosis | Fat-density mass ± rim/eggshell calcification; correlate with history of surgery or trauma |
| High-grade carcinoma | Round, circumscribed, but interval growth, posterior enhancement, vascularity, clinical context |

**Pitfalls:**
- *Summation pseudomasses* on 2D — confirmed absent on DBT and spot compression.
- *Skin and nipple lesions* projected into breast tissue — skin markers, tangential views, and ultrasound clarify the dermal origin.
- *Obscured margins* over-called as suspicious in dense tissue — DBT delineates the true margin.

### Calcifications

Calcification analysis is one of the areas where systematic thinking is most rewarding — the underlying biology directly predicts the morphology.

**Mammographic analysis:**

- *Morphology first:* benign forms include skin calcifications (lucent centre, "train-track"), vascular ("tram-track" following arterial walls), coarse "popcorn" (involuting fibroadenoma), rim/eggshell (fat necrosis, cyst wall), large rod-like secretory (duct ectasia, radiating from nipple), and milk of calcium. Suspicious forms include fine pleomorphic and fine linear/branching ("casting") — these reflect necrotic debris within distended ducts, characteristic of DCIS and occasionally invasive carcinoma.
- *Distribution second:* diffuse/scattered or bilateral distribution strongly favours benign aetiology. Grouped (five or more in 1 cm²), linear (along a duct), and segmental (conforming to a ductal system) distributions raise concern, especially when combined with suspicious morphology.
- *Evolution:* new or enlarging clusters require action; stable calcifications over many years are reassuring.

Always use **magnification views** to refine morphology — standard digital views at standard scale are insufficient for calcification characterisation.

**Ultrasound correlation:** Fine DCIS-related calcifications often have no ultrasound correlate. Coarser calcifications may appear as echogenic foci ± posterior shadowing within a duct. The absence of an ultrasound correlate for suspicious calcifications does not reduce the need for biopsy — it simply means stereotactic or tomosynthesis guidance is required.

**Biopsy:** Use stereotactic or tomosynthesis-guided vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB). Specimen radiography is mandatory to confirm retrieval of representative calcifications.

**Pitfalls:**
- *Skin calcifications* — use a BB marker and tangential view.
- *Vascular calcifications* — bilateral, following arterial trajectories, not ductal paths; check on MLO where vessels run differently to ducts.
- *Milk of calcium* — "teacup" appearance on true lateral (dependent layering); amorphous on CC. Obtain a 90-degree lateral view if in doubt; this is a benign diagnosis that does not require biopsy.

---

### Architectural distortion

Architectural distortion is defined as a focal disruption of the normal tissue architecture — radiating lines and focal retraction — without a definable central mass. It is arguably the most commonly missed mammographic finding, because it can be subtle and may blend into fibroglandular tissue on standard 2D views.

**DBT has transformed the detection of architectural distortion** — it substantially increases conspicuity by eliminating overlapping tissue. If you are working with 2D-only images and see potential distortion, have a low threshold for requesting DBT or spot compression views.

**Underlying biology:** Malignant distortion arises from infiltrative growth and reactive desmoplastic stromal change. Benign causes include radial scar/complex sclerosing lesion, post-surgical scar, sclerosing adenosis, and focal fibrosis.

**Ultrasound correlation:** Distortion may show as a subtle hypoechoic area with acoustic shadowing or architectural disruption, or may be occult on ultrasound. Use minimal transducer pressure — excessive pressure can compress the finding out of existence. Ultrasound is unreliable for excluding distortion; a negative ultrasound does not negate the need for biopsy of a suspicious mammographic distortion.

**Management:** New or progressive distortion without a corresponding surgical scar warrants biopsy. Tomosynthesis- or stereotactic-guided VAB is preferred as it samples adequately and allows radiological–pathological correlation. Radial scars are upgraded to carcinoma or atypical hyperplasia in a meaningful proportion of cases at excision — local policy varies on whether VAB suffices or surgical excision is required, and you should know your unit's approach.

**Pitfalls:**
- *Overlapping Cooper's ligaments* mimicking distortion — confirm on DBT, spot compression, and across multiple slices.
- *Post-surgical scars* — stable or regressing over time, aligned with the incision, ± skin retraction. New distortion at an old lumpectomy site should raise concern for recurrence.

---

### Asymmetries

Asymmetries are areas where one breast shows greater density than the equivalent area on the other side, without the features of a true mass.

| Type | Definition | Clinical significance |
|---|---|---|
| Global asymmetry | One quadrant or more | Usually normal variant or hormonal; compare with priors |
| Focal asymmetry | Small area seen on two views | Incomplete summation vs early mass; needs clarification |
| Developing asymmetry | New or enlarging compared with prior | More concerning; investigate |

The key steps are to confirm two-view persistence (not a summation artefact), use DBT or spot compression to determine whether a mass is concealed within, and correlate with ultrasound. Many asymmetries resolve as overlapping tissue on spot compression; others reveal a subtle mass requiring biopsy.

**Do not dismiss a developing asymmetry** — the temporal change is the most important feature. Comparing systematically with priors is what identifies these.

## Modality-specific systematic reading

### Mammography and DBT: a repeatable checklist

1. Patient identity, indication, prior studies present and reviewed.
2. Technical adequacy — view completeness, positioning, compression, artefacts.
3. Global sweep — symmetry, density, skin, nipple, axilla.
4. Compartment-by-compartment lesion search, bilateral.
5. Classify each finding (mass, calcifications, distortion, asymmetry) and apply descriptors.
6. Associated features — skin/nipple retraction, trabecular thickening, oedema, axillary nodes, satellite lesions.
7. Request additional views as needed — spot compression, magnification, 90-degree lateral, tangential.
8. Assign M-score with documented rationale.

**Common technical pitfalls:**
- Posterior lesions cut off because the CC PNL is more than 1 cm shorter than the MLO PNL.
- Medial lesions under-represented on MLO — ensure the CC is adequate medially.
- Retroareolar lesions masked by the nipple — request nipple-in-profile views or use ultrasound.
- Bilateral axillae not assessed after finding a unilateral lesion.

### Ultrasound: a disciplined lesion-first and sweep approach

Begin with the symptomatic area or mammographic target, then perform a systematic quadrant sweep. For each lesion, document:
- Position (clockface, distance from nipple and from skin surface)
- Size in three dimensions
- Shape and margins
- Echo pattern and internal architecture
- Posterior acoustic features
- Orientation (parallel vs non-parallel)
- Vascularity on Doppler
- Relationship to Cooper's ligaments, ducts, pectoral fascia, and chest wall

Assign a U-score and record the reasoning. A U3 or higher finding warrants biopsy unless there is a clearly concordant benign mammographic correlate and the clinical picture fits.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Compressing away a subtle hypoechoic shadow — use minimal transducer pressure.
- Mistaking mobile debris in a cyst for solid tissue — observe in real time, try repositioning the patient, check for Doppler flow.
- Over-relying on posterior features — malignancy can enhance, shadow, or show no posterior change.
- Missing satellite lesions — survey the surrounding tissue around any index lesion.

### MRI: structure your read

1. **T2 first:** cysts, oedema, lymph nodes, post-surgical seromas, implant integrity.
2. **DCE subtractions and MIP:** identify and map all enhancing foci, noting BPE level.
3. **Classify each enhancing finding:**
   - *Focus* (<5 mm), *mass* (with shape, margin, and internal enhancement morphology), or *non-mass enhancement* (NME — described by distribution: focal, linear, segmental, regional, diffuse).
   - Kinetic curve: initial rise and delayed phase (persistent, plateau, or washout). Washout pattern is suspicious but not diagnostic; persistent enhancement does not exclude malignancy. Kinetics support morphological interpretation but should never override it.
4. **Associated features:** oedema, skin thickening, pectoral involvement, nipple enhancement, multicentricity, contralateral disease.
5. **Correlate with mammography and ultrasound:** most MRI findings that are clinically actionable should be localisable on targeted second-look ultrasound. This allows ultrasound-guided biopsy, which is simpler and more tolerable than MRI-guided biopsy.

**Common pitfalls:**
- High BPE generating false-positive findings — check the timing in the cycle and compare with prior MRI.
- Post-treatment changes complicating assessment — use a baseline post-operative MRI for comparison when available.
- NME is a challenging category; segmental linear distribution raises concern for DCIS, but benign proliferative change can produce similar patterns. Biopsy decisions should be made in the context of the full triple assessment, not on MRI alone.

## Triangulation and cross-modality correlation

### Localising a lesion across mammographic views

The CC view tells you medial–lateral position; the MLO view tells you superior–inferior position. Using the nipple as a reference point:

- A lesion seen superiorly on MLO will also appear superiorly on a true lateral view.
- Combining CC and MLO positions (e.g., medial and superior) defines a superomedial location.
- DBT adds the third dimension — depth from the skin surface — enabling you to locate the lesion on ultrasound more precisely.

Medial lesions tend to appear more anteriorly than their true depth due to medial compression on the MLO; lateral lesions tend to appear more posteriorly. Be aware of these projection effects when targeting ultrasound.

### Converting mammographic coordinates to ultrasound

Use the clockface position and distance from nipple derived from mammographic localisation. Confirm the depth from DBT. Superficial lesions will be immediately beneath the skin; deep lesions are adjacent to the pectoral fascia. If you cannot find a lesion on targeted ultrasound despite thorough searching, consider whether your localisation was correct, whether the lesion is ultrasound-occult (common for calcifications and some distortions), and whether stereotactic guidance is required.

### Concordance after biopsy

Imaging–pathology concordance is the final quality check. Ask: does the histological result *explain* the imaging?

- "Fibroadenoma" is concordant with a circumscribed oval hypoechoic mass in a young woman — manage conservatively.
- "Fibrocystic change" is *not* concordant with a mass with spiculated margins and a U4 score — re-biopsy or excise.
- "Radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion" is concordant with architectural distortion — but requires MDT discussion about upgrade risk and further management (VAB excision vs surgical excision, depending on lesion size and local protocol).

A discordant benign biopsy result is not reassuring — it requires prompt action, either repeat biopsy or surgical excision. **Imaging–pathology discordance is one of the most important concepts in breast radiology.**

B3 lesions (of uncertain malignant potential, including atypical ductal hyperplasia, lobular neoplasia, and flat epithelial atypia) require MDT discussion because upgrade rates vary by lesion type and adequacy of sampling. Practice varies between units — some favour vacuum excision for all B3 lesions; others stratify by specific B3 subtype and radiological features.

## Assigning UK scores and synthesising triple assessment

| Score | Meaning | Typical action |
|---|---|---|
| M/U 1 | Normal | No action in screening; correlate clinically if symptomatic |
| M/U 2 | Benign | Conservative management if concordant with P2 |
| M/U 3 | Indeterminate | Additional views, targeted ultrasound; often biopsy in screening recall or discordant symptoms |
| M/U 4 | Suspicious | Image-guided core biopsy |
| M/U 5 | Malignant | Biopsy for histological confirmation and receptor analysis before treatment planning |

The scores only make sense in combination. If P5 (clinically malignant) is paired with M2/U2, do not simply document the discordance and move on — actively investigate it. Is the imaging targeting the correct area? Is there an extramammary explanation for the palpable mass? Does the patient need MRI? Bring the case to MDT rather than resolving it unilaterally.

## Common diagnoses approached systematically

**Duct ectasia and secretory calcifications:** Rod-like, occasionally Y-shaped calcifications radiating toward the nipple, often bilateral. On ultrasound, dilated ducts with echogenic content. Typically M2/U2 if uncomplicated and the morphology is classic. Ductal asymmetry with suspicious associated features warrants biopsy.

**Radial scar versus carcinoma:** Both produce architectural distortion without a central mass. DBT improves detection markedly. Biopsy is required — these cannot be distinguished reliably on imaging. The upgrade rate to carcinoma or high-risk lesion at surgery is significant enough that local protocols often recommend vacuum excision rather than trucut biopsy alone, although practice varies.

**Post-operative change:** Expect oedema, skin thickening, and seroma in the early post-operative period. Fat necrosis develops later and evolves — oil cyst formation with rim calcification is characteristic and benign. New calcifications at a lumpectomy site can represent benign fat necrosis or recurrent/residual DCIS — interval change, morphology, and magnification views guide biopsy decisions.

**Implant assessment:** Assess for intracapsular rupture (linguine sign on MRI — collapsed elastomer shell within the silicone), extracapsular silicone, and capsular calcification. For cancer screening, mammography with implant-displaced views remains standard; ultrasound addresses focal symptoms.

## Technical and cognitive pitfalls: summary

| Pitfall | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Satisfaction of search | Deliberately restart systematic search after finding the first lesion |
| Anchoring on a benign diagnosis | Ensure all descriptors are formally addressed before concluding |
| Over-calling artefact | Confirm findings on two views, DBT slices, and spot compression |
| Underestimating developing change | Compare rigorously with all available priors |
| Ultrasound confirmation bias (fitting US to a benign narrative despite suspicious mammography) | Biopsy the mammographic target using appropriate guidance |
| Mis-triangulation | Practise CC–MLO conversion; use depth from DBT; mark palpable lesions |
| Density blind spots | Consider DBT and careful ultrasound in dense breasts; a normal mammogram does not exclude cancer in symptomatic dense-breasted women |
| Inadequate positioning | Feedback loops with radiographers; request repeats early |

## Reporting structure and communication

**Screening report:**
- Document technical adequacy and breast density.
- Note all actionable findings with type, side, quadrant, size, and reason for recall.
- Reader comments should be clear enough to support arbitration, noting DBT support where applicable.

**Assessment clinic report:**
- Describe mammographic findings and additional view results.
- Document ultrasound systematically with measurements and images.
- State M and U scores explicitly with brief rationale.
- State the proposed plan (biopsy, short-interval review, or discharge) tied to triple assessment concordance.

**Symptomatic clinic report:**
- Anchor findings to the patient's presenting complaint and P-score.
- Explain any imaging–clinical discordance.
- Recommend clear next steps.

**MDT presentation:**
- Present a cohesive narrative integrating P, M, U, and B scores.
- Highlight concordance or discordance and its implications.
- Know your unit's protocols for B3 subtypes and radial scars.

Precision in language matters. "Circumscribed oval hypoechoic mass, U2" is more useful than "probably benign cyst-like lesion." "Fine pleomorphic calcifications in segmental distribution, M4, recommend stereotactic VAB" is more useful than "suspicious calcifications, biopsy considered."

## Special situations

**Pregnancy and lactation:** Ultrasound is the first-line modality. Mammography is acceptable with abdominal shielding when the clinical situation demands it. Expect increased vascularity, dilated ducts, and increased density. Galactoceles can appear solid if proteinaceous — aspiration confirms the diagnosis.

**Male breast:** Subareolar gynaecomastia (fan-shaped, flame-shaped, or nodular) is usually benign. An eccentric solid mass, particularly in an older man, raises concern for carcinoma. Ultrasound aids targeting; axillary nodes should be assessed as in female patients.

**High-risk screening with MRI:** BPE can be substantial and creates significant interpretation challenges. Correlate back to mammography systematically to plan targeted second-look ultrasound. In high-risk women, new or rapidly enhancing lesions warrant a lower threshold for biopsy than in the general population.

## Practical quick-reference checklists

### Mammography/DBT
- [ ] Patient, indication, priors reviewed
- [ ] Technical adequacy: PNL match, muscle inclusion, IMF, nipple, artefacts
- [ ] Global sweep: symmetry, density, skin, nipple, axilla
- [ ] Systematic lesion search, both breasts
- [ ] Classify and analyse: mass / calcifications / distortion / asymmetry
- [ ] Associated features noted
- [ ] Additional views obtained as needed
- [ ] M-score assigned with rationale

### Ultrasound
- [ ] Target symptomatic/mammographic area first, then survey
- [ ] Technical settings optimised; minimal pressure
- [ ] Each lesion documented: position, size, shape, margins, echo pattern, posterior features, orientation, vascularity
- [ ] U-score assigned with rationale
- [ ] Concordance with mammography confirmed or discordance flagged

### MRI
- [ ] Protocol and timing confirmed
- [ ] T2 reviewed: cysts, oedema, nodes, seromas, implants
- [ ] DCE: foci/masses/NME identified, morphology and distribution described, kinetics noted
- [ ] BPE level recorded
- [ ] Correlated with mammography and ultrasound
- [ ] Management pathway clearly stated

## Conclusion

A systematic approach is not a bureaucratic imposition — it is your safety net. Begin with technical adequacy and clinical context; take a global view before zooming in; search methodically by compartment; classify findings precisely; correlate across modalities; and anchor your conclusions in UK triple assessment. Seek imaging–pathology concordance after every biopsy — it is the point at which systematic interpretation meets real-world consequences.

Acknowledge where the evidence supports firm conclusions and where it demands judgement, and do not be afraid to say so at MDT. With deliberate practice and reflection on your misses and near-misses, the system becomes both faster and safer — a transferable scaffold that holds even when the case in front of you is unlike anything you have seen before.
