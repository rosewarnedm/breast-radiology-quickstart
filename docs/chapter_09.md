# Chapter 9: Correlation with Ultrasound and Biopsy

---

## Why Correlation Matters

Triple assessment only works when its three components — clinical examination, imaging, and tissue diagnosis — are genuinely integrated. Correlation is the active, deliberate process of checking that your imaging finding, your sonographic target, and your histological result are describing the same lesion. It is not a clerical formality; it is a safety step.

Getting it right has direct consequences. A concordant benign result on a circumscribed mass allows safe discharge. A discordant benign result on a spiculated mass means you have almost certainly missed a cancer, and you must act on that. Good correlation also ensures you are guiding a needle to the correct place before you put it in — not afterwards.

The UK framework is the P/M/U/B scoring system. Think of it as a shared language across the MDT: clinical findings scored P1–5, mammography M1–5, ultrasound U1–5, and histology B1–5. A final management decision should be based on the aggregate and on concordance between scores, not on any one result in isolation.

## 1. Fundamentals of Image–Pathology Correlation

### 1.1 The Three Questions

Before accepting any biopsy result, answer these explicitly:

1. **Did I sample the right target?** Confirm that the needle trajectory and biopsy site match the imaging coordinates of the lesion.
2. **Does the pathology fit the imaging phenotype?** A fibroadenoma (B2) should look like a fibroadenoma on imaging. A "fibrocystic change" result for a spiculated mass does not fit.
3. **Is the level of certainty sufficient to act?** A B1 (inadequate/normal) result for any imaging abnormality scored M3 or above is not an acceptable endpoint.

If any answer is uncertain, escalate: repeat sampling, change guidance modality, perform vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB), or bring to MDT before a management decision is made.

### 1.2 Why the Physics Matters

You cannot correlate what you do not understand.

**Mammography** detects differences in X-ray attenuation across compressed, overlapping tissue. Masses and calcifications become conspicuous, but summation artefact (superimposed normal parenchyma) can mimic or obscure pathology. Projection geometry means a lesion's apparent position shifts between views.

**Ultrasound** detects acoustic interfaces and tissue echogenicity. It is excellent for characterising masses and guiding needles in real time, but microcalcifications are poorly visualised unless coarse, clustered, or embedded within an echo-bright mass or duct. Technique errors — beam angle, compression, transducer frequency — directly affect what you see and what you miss.

**Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT)** reduces tissue overlap, refines morphology, and localises lesions within a slab. It is increasingly the standard at assessment, and understanding its slice geometry is necessary before you can translate a mammographic finding to a sonographic search.

Understanding these differences is the prerequisite for everything that follows.

## 2. From Mammogram to Ultrasound: A Systematic Approach

### 2.1 Build a Precise Localisation Description

Before scanning, convert the mammographic finding into a structured search plan. You need:

- **Side** and **clock-face position** (always document using the position with the patient supine, as for ultrasound — 12 o'clock is superior regardless of how the breast falls on the mammogram)
- **Distance from nipple (DFN)** in centimetres
- **Depth**: anterior (subcutaneous/prechondral), middle (fibroglandular), or posterior (retroglandular) third
- **Size** on two mammographic views
- **DBT slice depth** from skin or detector, if available — know your unit's slice-order convention (tube-side versus detector-side)
- **Relationship to landmarks**: surgical scar marker, pectoral muscle, vascular tracks

Write this down in the assessment proforma and carry it through to the ultrasound and biopsy documentation. If you cannot articulate where the lesion is before you scan, you are already correlating badly.

### 2.2 Triangulating Between Mammographic Views

The CC view tells you medial/lateral position. The MLO tells you superior/inferior position — but with a parallax error for oblique compression. When you have a true lateral (ML or LM), use it: it removes that parallax and gives you the most accurate vertical position.

If you only have CC and MLO, apply the standard rule: **medial lesions appear relatively high on the MLO; lateral lesions appear relatively low** — because the oblique compression pulls lateral tissue inferiorly. This helps you avoid searching in entirely the wrong quadrant.

DBT depth translates to ultrasound zone. Allow for the fact that mammographic compression redistributes tissue: a lesion appearing posteriorly on a compressed view may sit more anteriorly in the uncompressed breast. Make this allowance when planning your scan depth.

### 2.3 Executing the Ultrasound Search

Position the patient to approximate the mammographic geometry. For most lesions, the patient lies supine with the ipsilateral arm abducted; rotate slightly contralaterally for lateral lesions, ipsilaterally for medial ones. This reduces the distance from lesion to skin and flattens the gland.

Begin with a **targeted sweep** through the predicted sector at the predicted DFN, scanning in both radial/antiradial and transverse/longitudinal planes. Apply light initial pressure — over-compression effaces soft masses. Adjust frequency and focal zone to the predicted depth.

If the mammographic finding is ductal or segmental (such as linear calcifications), trace the duct from the nipple outwards. Ductal architecture on ultrasound follows a radial pattern, so scan radially when you suspect an intraductal lesion.

If after a careful, methodical, time-adequate search you find no confident correlate, stop and reconsider the biopsy route — the lesion may be mammography-only, and that changes everything.

## 3. Choosing the Right Guidance Modality

### 3.1 The Principle

Use the modality that most directly and accurately targets the lesion you are trying to biopsy. Do not let convenience override accuracy.

| Imaging finding | Preferred guidance |
|---|---|
| Any sonographically visible target | Ultrasound-guided core or VAB |
| Calcifications without US correlate | Stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB |
| Architectural distortion without US correlate | DBT-guided VAB |
| MRI-only lesion | MRI-guided biopsy (designated centre) |

### 3.2 When Not to Use Ultrasound

Suspicious calcifications without a confident sonographic match should not be sampled under ultrasound guidance — you cannot confirm you are in the right place. Similarly, architectural distortion with no US correlate requires stereotactic or DBT guidance. Equivocal asymmetry that effaces entirely on spot compression or DBT review probably does not need biopsy at all, but if it persists and has no US target, proceed under mammographic guidance.

Critically: **do not biopsy an adjacent lesion as a substitute target.** If the suspicious mammographic finding persists on imaging, it requires its own biopsy under the appropriate modality.

## 4. Ultrasound Technique for Correlation

### 4.1 Image Optimisation

Use a high-frequency linear probe (12–18 MHz for most lesions; drop to 9–12 MHz for deep or posterior targets). Place the focal zone at or just below the lesion. Narrow the field of view to increase line density. Tissue harmonic imaging often improves margin conspicuity. Compound imaging can smooth apparent margins and obscure fine spiculation — use it selectively.

### 4.2 Recognising Mammographic Features on Ultrasound

| Mammographic finding | Expected US correlate |
|---|---|
| Circumscribed mass | Oval, parallel, circumscribed hypoechoic or isoechoic mass; posterior enhancement if fibroadenoma/cyst |
| Spiculated mass | Irregular, non-parallel, angular hypoechoic mass; posterior shadowing; echogenic halo corresponding to desmoplastic spicules |
| Architectural distortion | Subtle hypoechoic area; straightened Cooper's ligaments converging centrally; focal shadowing without a discrete mass |
| Suspicious calcifications | Often invisible; sometimes echogenic foci within a hypoechoic duct or mass; coarse dystrophic calcifications more conspicuous |
| Focal asymmetry | Often summation — normal fibroglandular pattern on US; if abnormal, a subtle focal mass or architectural change |

### 4.3 Common Pitfalls

- **Anisotropy**: fibrous tissue appears hypoechoic when the beam strikes obliquely. Fan the transducer to confirm persistence.
- **Over-compression**: a soft or gelatinous mass (mucinous carcinoma, haematoma) can disappear entirely. Start with light pressure.
- **Accepting the wrong target**: a fibroadenoma in the predicted quadrant is not automatically the mammographic finding unless the DFN and depth genuinely match.
- **Missing posterior or tail-of-Spence lesions**: adjust patient positioning and probe orientation; posterior lesions are easily missed by inexperienced operators.
- **Low-conspicuity invasive lobular carcinoma**: manifests as subtle tethering or a vague hypoechoic region. If clinical or mammographic suspicion is high and US is negative, proceed to DBT-guided biopsy or consider MRI.

## 5. Lesion-specific Correlation and Biopsy Approach

### 5.1 Spiculated Masses

These carry a high pre-test probability of malignancy. A US correlate is usually present and should be found with careful technique. Use US-guided 14G core biopsy (three to five passes); for small lesions near the chest wall, VAB provides more tissue and better sampling. Place a marker clip routinely. If the lesion is visible only on DBT, proceed to DBT-guided VAB.

### 5.2 Circumscribed Masses

Likely benign (fibroadenoma, cyst, lymph node) but not exclusively so — mucinous carcinoma, medullary-like carcinoma, papillary carcinoma, and some triple-negative tumours can all appear circumscribed. A classic thin-walled anechoic cyst with posterior enhancement needs no biopsy unless symptomatic or haemorrhagic. For solid circumscribed masses in the screening-age group, UK practice is generally to biopsy at first presentation, but this varies between centres; use the MDT and patient preference to guide you. Younger women with classic fibroadenoma features (U2, M2) are sometimes managed with imaging surveillance, particularly in the symptomatic setting.

### 5.3 Architectural Distortion

If a US correlate is found, VAB is preferable to standard core biopsy because architectural distortion carries a significant upgrade risk (radial scars and complex sclerosing lesions can harbour ADH, DCIS, or invasive carcinoma). If no US correlate, biopsy under DBT guidance (VAB preferred). Place a clip in all cases — these lesions can be impossible to re-localise without one.

### 5.4 Suspicious Calcifications

Pleomorphic, fine-linear, or fine-linear-branching calcifications in segmental or linear distribution are high-risk and should be biopsied with stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB. Take a generous number of passes (typically six to twelve or more depending on gauge and extent). **Specimen radiography is mandatory** — if calcification retrieval is not confirmed on the specimen film, you cannot know whether you sampled the right area. Place a clip before the procedure ends, because you may inadvertently remove the entire cluster.

Probably-benign calcifications (round/punctate, skin, vascular, dystrophic, milk-of-calcium) should be characterised by morphology and distribution and correlated with the overall mammographic score. If confidently M1 or M2, biopsy is not required. If uncertainty persists, biopsy under stereotactic/DBT guidance rather than ultrasound unless there is a well-matched sonographic target.

### 5.5 Mammographic Asymmetries

An asymmetry that effaces on spot compression or DBT review is almost certainly summation artefact — no biopsy is warranted. A persistent focal asymmetry with associated distortion or suspicious features, and no US correlate, should be sampled under DBT guidance. Do not conclude that an ultrasound examination is negative for an asymmetry without genuinely confirming that your scan covered the correct coordinates.

### 5.6 Retroareolar and Papillary Lesions

Intraductal masses with or without duct dilatation are most common in the retroareolar region. US-guided VAB provides a higher diagnostic yield than 14G core for papillary lesions and may be therapeutic for benign papillomas (though practice varies). If calcifications are present in a retroareolar duct, stereotactic VAB is needed even if a papilloma is found on US — DCIS can coexist.

### 5.7 Post-treatment Changes and Fat Necrosis

Oil cysts, rim calcifications, and architectural distortion following surgery or radiotherapy can closely mimic recurrence. Correlate meticulously with clinical history, operative notes, and the timing of changes. When doubt persists after imaging review, biopsy — VAB for distortion or calcification. On MRI, enhancing rims in the irradiated breast can represent fat necrosis or recurrence; the time course since treatment and comparison with prior MRI helps, but biopsy remains the definitive step.

### 5.8 Axillary Lymph Nodes

Features warranting sampling: cortical thickening >3 mm (focal or diffuse), eccentric cortical bulge, focal or complete loss of fatty hilum, rounded morphology, non-hilar vascularity, calcifications (particularly in HER2-positive disease). Practice varies between FNA and 14G core; many UK centres prefer core for higher sensitivity and the ability to perform immunohistochemistry. Record the nodal level sampled. If neoadjuvant chemotherapy is planned and the node is positive, clip it for targeted axillary dissection.

Note: lymph node histology is reported with its own terminology (metastasis, micrometastasis, isolated tumour cells) — B-codes apply to breast parenchymal cores.

### 5.9 Implants

Use Eklund (implant-displacement) views in screening where possible. On ultrasound, adjust technique to avoid reverberation artefact. Biopsy of peri-implant lesions requires care: use a shallow, oblique approach, short needle throws, and remain well away from the implant shell. Consider MRI for problem-solving when correlation remains uncertain after mammography and ultrasound.

## 6. Biopsy Techniques in Practice

### 6.1 Ultrasound-guided Core Biopsy

Standard gauge is 14G with an automated gun; throw length 15–22 mm depending on lesion size and proximity to the chest wall. Infiltrate local anaesthetic subcutaneously, along the track, and around the lesion; hydrodissection using anaesthetic solution can separate a deep lesion from the chest wall and reduce pneumothorax risk.

Take three to five cores for most masses, more if the lesion is heterogeneous or if additional tissue is needed for immunohistochemistry (e.g., suspected papillary or mucinous lesion). Use a parallel, long-axis approach to the lesion where possible, and document the needle traversing the target in real time on a saved image. Place a marker clip for cancers, B3 lesions, and any lesion that may require surgical localisation.

### 6.2 Vacuum-assisted Biopsy (VAB) and Vacuum-assisted Excision (VAE)

VAB uses continuous suction to draw tissue into a larger-gauge needle (typically 8–10G), allowing contiguous sampling of a greater volume with a single insertion. Indications include small masses needing larger samples, papillary lesions, radial scars, and B3 lesions. VAE (7–10G) can achieve complete sonographic removal of small lesions and is used therapeutically for selected benign papillomas and in some B3 management protocols. Obtain a post-procedure mammogram if calcifications were visualised sonographically, to document removal and clip position.

### 6.3 Stereotactic/DBT-guided VAB

The standard approach for calcifications and architectural distortion without a US correlate. Prone or upright systems are used; DBT guidance reduces targeting time for distortions compared with conventional 2D stereotaxis. Take an adequate number of cores (six to twelve or more, depending on gauge and the extent of calcifications). Specimen radiography is **not optional** — it confirms whether calcifications have been retrieved. Site a clip before removing the probe.

### 6.4 MRI-guided Biopsy

Reserved for lesions visible on MRI only, where the finding would change management. Availability is limited in the UK; refer to regional centres as per local pathways. The lesion must be confirmed to enhance at the time of biopsy — background parenchymal enhancement varies with the menstrual cycle and can alter conspicuity. Vacuum-assisted devices are preferred.

### 6.5 Anticoagulation and Consent

Follow local policy for anticoagulant and antiplatelet management — most centres proceed with US-guided core on aspirin alone; clopidogrel and DOACs typically require a bridge or a period of omission. Weigh the bleeding risk against the harm of diagnostic delay.

Consent should cover: bruising and haematoma, pain, infection, vasovagal reaction, and the rare risk of pneumothorax for chest wall-adjacent lesions. Mention the very low risk of milk fistula in lactating women, and the negligible risk of titanium allergy from clips.

## 7. Interpreting Histology: The B-code System and Concordance

### 7.1 The B-codes

| Code | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | Normal tissue or inadequate sample | Re-sample if imaging is ≥M3 or ≥U3 |
| B2 | Benign | Accept if concordant; escalate if discordant |
| B3 | Uncertain malignant potential (ADH, FEA, lobular neoplasia, radial scar, papilloma ± atypia, mucocoele-like lesion) | MDT — often VAE or diagnostic surgery |
| B4 | Suspicious but not diagnostic | Repeat biopsy (VAB) or diagnostic excision |
| B5a | In situ malignancy | Definitive management planning |
| B5b | Invasive malignancy | Full staging, receptor assessment |

### 7.2 Judging Concordance

This is the most important intellectual step in the correlation process. Ask yourself: does this histological result plausibly explain what I was looking at on imaging?

- **Concordant**: oval circumscribed mass (U2, M2) → fibroadenoma (B2). Reasonable to manage with short-interval follow-up or discharge per MDT.
- **Concordant B3**: DBT-detected architectural distortion → radial scar (B3). This is expected, but the upgrade risk means you cannot simply discharge — manage per local B3 protocol, usually VAE or surgery.
- **Discordant**: spiculated mass (M5, U5) → fibrocystic change or fatty tissue (B2). This is an urgent failure of correlation. Resample immediately with VAB or an alternative modality, and discuss at MDT.
- **Discordant — technical failure**: segmental pleomorphic calcifications → benign secretory calcifications, but no calcification on specimen radiograph. The sample did not contain the target. Resample.

**Document concordance explicitly** in your report. If it is discordant, say so, state your plan, and ensure MDT review happens promptly. A culture of naming discordance is a patient safety culture.

## 8. Post-biopsy Marking and Localisation

A marker clip should be placed:

- For any confirmed or suspected malignancy
- For any mammography-only lesion
- For B3 lesions
- Before neoadjuvant systemic therapy
- Whenever the lesion may be difficult to relocate (cystic, soft-tissue distortion, post-VAB cavity)

Document clip deployment with an immediate post-procedure image (ultrasound or mammogram). Early post-deployment mammography documents the baseline clip position before any migration can occur — clip migration is more likely in loose stroma or after large-cavity VAB.

Preoperative localisation should be planned using the modality that most reliably visualises the clip or lesion: ultrasound where possible; stereotactic/DBT hookwire, magnetic seed (Magseed), or radar reflector (SAVI SCOUT) for mammography-only targets. Bracketing (multiple markers/wires) is appropriate for extensive calcification or distortion. Ensure compatibility between the chosen localisation device, intraoperative detection equipment, and MRI (if post-operative MRI is planned).

## 9. Quality Assurance and Governance in the NHSBSP

Quality is not incidental — it is embedded in the NHSBSP structure. As a trainee, you should understand what is being measured and why.

- **Audit targets include**: VAB upgrade rates for B3 lesions, false-negative rates for US-guided cores, calcification retrieval adequacy, clip placement accuracy, and re-biopsy rates for discordant results.
- **Double reading and arbitration** at screening reduce both recall errors and missed cancers; at assessment, a documented correlation plan is the equivalent safeguard.
- **Interval cancer review** and **peer review of assessment misses** are learning opportunities — studying cases where correlation failed is the most efficient way to avoid repeating them.
- **Advanced practitioners** (radiographers performing assessment ultrasound and biopsies) work within standardised protocols under supervision; maintaining and auditing their competency is a governance responsibility shared with the radiology team.

## 10. Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

| Pitfall | Prevention |
|---|---|
| Sampling the wrong lesion | Reconcile DFN, clock position, and depth between mammography/DBT and US before inserting any needle |
| Accepting a nearby benign lesion as a substitute target | If the suspicious mammographic finding persists, it requires its own biopsy under the appropriate guidance |
| Under-sampling calcifications | Take abundant VAB cores; specimen radiography is mandatory; document retrieval |
| Missing soft masses through over-compression | Begin every US examination with light transducer pressure |
| Missing satellite foci once a cancer is found | Re-survey the whole breast and axilla; correlate with DBT to exclude synchronous lesions |
| Lobular cancers appearing negative on US | Maintain a low threshold for DBT-guided biopsy or MRI when clinical/mammographic suspicion is high |
| Not acting on discordance | Build an explicit habit of stating concordance in every report; bring all discordant cases to MDT without delay |

## 11. Key Differentials and Correlative Clues

**Fibroadenoma versus mucinous carcinoma**: Both are circumscribed and hypoechoic; mucinous carcinoma can show exaggerated posterior acoustic enhancement and tends to occur in the screening age group. Biopsy any new circumscribed mass that does not have a confident prior comparison showing longstanding stability.

**Papilloma versus DCIS in retroareolar ducts**: Papilloma has a vascular stalk visible on Doppler, and is a discrete intraductal mass; DCIS more often presents with calcification and segmental duct change. If calcifications are present anywhere in the duct, stereotactic VAB is required regardless of what the US-guided biopsy shows.

**Radial scar versus invasive carcinoma**: Both cause architectural distortion on mammography and DBT. VAB improves characterisation, but even a confirmed B3 radial scar carries an upgrade risk and mandates further management — it is not a benign endpoint equivalent to B2.

**Fat necrosis versus recurrence**: Post-treatment history is critical context. Oil cysts and rim calcifications developing in the years after radiotherapy are almost always fat necrosis. New or enlarging solid components, or interval change on MRI, should prompt biopsy. Do not rely on MRI enhancement alone in an irradiated breast — correlate with the time course.

## 12. A Correlation Workflow: Pulling It Together

1. **Review mammography/DBT**: Assign M score. Describe the finding with precise localisation descriptors (clock position, DFN, depth, size). Note DBT slice depth.
2. **Perform targeted ultrasound**: Assign U score. Decide whether there is a confident, anatomically plausible correlate.
3. **Choose guidance modality**:
   - Confident US correlate → US-guided core biopsy or VAB
   - No US correlate → DBT-guided VAB; or MRI-guided biopsy for MRI-only lesions
4. **Biopsy**: Adequate number and gauge of cores; document needle in target in real time; specimen radiography for all calcification biopsies; clip as indicated.
5. **Histology returned**: B-code assigned.
6. **Concordance check**: Does the B-code plausibly explain the imaging? If yes, manage per MDT. If no, re-image, resample, or escalate — and document your reasoning.
7. **Localisation planning**: Based on lesion visibility and confirmed clip position.
8. **MDT**: Integrate P, M, U, B, and axillary status (and MRI where performed) to reach a final management decision.

## Key Take-home Principles

- **Let the mammogram and DBT direct your ultrasound search.** Precise localisation before you scan is not optional.
- **If you cannot find a confident US correlate, change the guidance modality — not the target.** The mammographic finding still needs to be biopsied.
- **Judge every histology result against the imaging phenotype.** Concordance is an active clinical judgement, not a box-tick.
- **Use VAB generously** for calcifications, distortion, and B3 lesions. Confirm calcification retrieval on every specimen radiograph.
- **Mark what you biopsy** if there is any chance the lesion may shrink, prove difficult to relocate, or require surgical excision.
- **Name discordance explicitly and act on it promptly.** A culture of honest correlation review is what keeps patients safe.
