# Chapter 12: Case-Based Learning

## How to use this chapter

This chapter works through realistic cases from both the NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) and the symptomatic breast clinic, tracing each from presentation through imaging, interpretation, triple assessment, biopsy, and management. The aim is to teach *principles* — how pathology reshapes anatomy and therefore imaging appearances — rather than isolated facts.

A few anchoring points apply throughout:

- **NHSBSP** invites women aged 50–70 for two-view digital mammography every three years, with double reading and arbitration. Age-extension pilots exist; local practice varies.
- **Triple assessment** underpins diagnosis in both screening and symptomatic contexts: clinical examination scored P1–P5, mammography M1–M5, ultrasound U1–U5, and histopathology B1–B5. *Concordance across these four elements is the governing principle.* A discordant benign biopsy result in an M4/U4 lesion is not a safe endpoint.
- **Modality roles**: digital mammography (± DBT) for screening and assessment; ultrasound to characterise masses and guide biopsy; MRI selectively for problem-solving, extent mapping, and occult disease. DBT is now standard in assessment clinics in most UK units.
- **Biopsy techniques**: ultrasound-guided 14G core for sonographic masses; stereotactic or DBT-guided vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB) for calcifications and distortion; MRI-guided biopsy for MRI-only lesions that would alter management. Always place a clip and obtain a post-biopsy mammogram to document targeting.

Each case follows a consistent structure: clinical context → imaging findings → reasoning and differentials → UK scoring → biopsy and pathology → pitfalls and pearls → key learning points. Where practice legitimately varies, options are stated with rationale.

## Case 12.1: Screen-detected spiculated mass — invasive carcinoma

### Clinical context
Fifty-six-year-old woman, routine NHSBSP, asymptomatic. Both readers recall for a left upper outer quadrant lesion; arbitration is not required.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Irregular spiculated mass, 10 o'clock, posterior third, approximately 12 mm. High density relative to surrounding fibroglandular tissue. Fine radiating spicules with subtle focal architectural distortion; no calcifications; possible minimal skin retraction. Background density: scattered.

**DBT at assessment:** Confirms a discrete irregular mass with long spicules; surrounding tissue overlap is resolved, confirming no satellite lesions.

**Ultrasound:** 11 mm hypoechoic irregular mass, non-parallel orientation, angular margins, posterior acoustic shadowing, echogenic halo. Internal and rim vascularity on Doppler. One level I axillary node with mild eccentric cortical thickening (3.5 mm) but a preserved fatty hilum.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

The key mechanistic principle here is **desmoplasia**: invasive carcinoma triggers a stromal fibrotic response that retracts Cooper's ligaments, producing the spiculated margin visible on mammography. This is a highly specific malignant signature — once you understand why spiculation occurs, it is harder to dismiss.

The differential is narrow: invasive carcinoma (no special type, NST, or a special type such as tubular) versus **radial scar/complex sclerosing lesion (CSL)**. Both can produce architectural distortion with posterior acoustic shadowing on ultrasound. Features favouring carcinoma here are: a definite mass centre on DBT (rather than pure distortion without a mass), internal and rim vascularity, and the patient's age. Features that would favour a radial scar are a central radiolucency, thin straight long spicules, and absence of a mass on DBT — the so-called "black star" appearance.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M5, U5**
- Ultrasound-guided 14G core biopsy, 3–4 cores; clip placed.
- The axillary node: practice varies nationally. Many units biopsy a node with focal cortical thickening ≥3 mm; others use ≥4 mm as the threshold. Follow your local protocol, document your reasoning, and discuss in the MDT if borderline.

### Pathology

- **B5b** invasive carcinoma of no special type, grade 2; ER positive, PR positive, HER2 negative.
- If node was biopsied and is B2, this guides surgical planning; if not biopsied, axillary staging proceeds at surgery.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Posterior-third masses can be visible on only one mammographic view — always scrutinise both projections and use DBT.
- If stereotactic features are equivocal between radial scar and carcinoma, proceed to VAB; a 14G core may undersample a CSL and miss associated atypia or malignancy.
- Small spiculated cancers in low-density breasts are easily overlooked against fibrous ligaments; train your eye to search systematically.

### Key learning points

- Spiculation arises from desmoplasia retracting breast stroma: understanding the mechanism makes the sign memorable and less likely to be dismissed.
- DBT at assessment confirms a true mass and reduces false positives from tissue summation.
- Ultrasound-guided core is first-line for a sonographic mass; always correlate the B result with M and U scores (concordance check).

## Case 12.2: Focal asymmetry vs normal tissue — avoiding over-recall

### Clinical context
Fifty-two-year-old woman, routine screening, prevalent round (first screen). First reader recalls for new focal asymmetry on the right CC view; second reader considers summation; arbitration advises assessment.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Triangular focal asymmetry, retroareolar, middle third, right CC view. No defined border, no associated distortion, no calcifications. No corresponding focality on the MLO.

**Problem-solving views at assessment:**
- Spot compression CC: asymmetry disperses.
- Rolled CC views: density shifts with rolling, consistent with summation from overlapping structures.

**DBT:** Layered interdigitating fibroglandular tissue; no discrete mass or distortion.

**Ultrasound:** Normal echotexture; ducts <2 mm, compressible; no focal lesion.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

The retroareolar region is anatomically prone to tissue overlap — ducts converge there and fibrous strands cross. A focal asymmetry on a single view, without corresponding abnormality on the orthogonal projection, that disperses with spot compression and shifts predictably with rolling, is behaving like **summation artefact**. DBT resolves the overlap into separate anatomical planes and confirms there is no mass or true distortion. Concordant normal ultrasound completes the picture.

The contrast is important: a real focal asymmetry would *persist* on spot compression, remain in a fixed position despite rolling (it would move *with* the tissue rather than relative to positional landmarks), and might have a correlate on DBT or ultrasound.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M2, U1**
- Reassurance; return to routine three-yearly recall.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- If spot compression *does not* disperse the asymmetry, or if there is any associated architectural distortion, further work-up (DBT if not already done, ultrasound, possible biopsy) is warranted.
- Prior screening images are invaluable — stability over years is powerful evidence of benignity. Prevalent-round participants have no prior images, so the threshold for assessment may reasonably be slightly lower.
- Rolled views are a simple, low-cost problem-solving tool; use them routinely for single-view findings.

### Key learning points

- Summation artefact is a frequent driver of recall; understanding the geometry of breast positioning explains why it arises.
- DBT and problem-solving mammographic views together substantially reduce unnecessary assessment and biopsy.
- Assign M2/U1 with confidence when all problem-solving manoeuvres are concordantly benign.

## Case 12.3: Calcifications — milk of calcium vs DCIS

### Clinical context
Sixty-year-old woman, routine screening recall for left lower inner quadrant calcifications.

### Imaging findings

**Standard mammography:** Segmental distribution of microcalcifications spanning approximately 25 mm, lower inner quadrant, middle third. On standard CC and MLO: smudgy, amorphous to punctate morphology.

**Magnification views:**
- CC magnification: smudgy and amorphous forms.
- ML (true lateral) magnification: *layering*, dependent crescentic or "teacup" shapes within microcysts.

**DBT:** Clustered microcysts with dependent material; no associated mass or distortion.

**Ultrasound:** Often insensitive for microcalcifications; here, scattered microcysts with echogenic foci and comet-tail artefacts. No ductal ectasia or associated mass.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

The underlying mechanism of **milk of calcium** is sedimentation of calcium salts in fluid within benign microcysts. Because gravity acts on the calcium, its shape changes with the angle of the X-ray beam: on the CC view (beam horizontal relative to the cyst), calcium projects as a smudgy circle; on the true lateral or ML (beam perpendicular to gravity), calcium settles dependently and produces a crescentic or teacup shape. This *projectional shape change* is pathognomonic for benignity.

The critical differential is **amorphous or punctate DCIS calcifications**: these are also small and poorly defined, but their morphology does *not* change with projection because the calcium is deposited within ductal secretions, not suspended in cyst fluid. DCIS calcifications also tend to maintain their shape. Distribution alone (segmental) might raise the index of suspicion, but morphological behaviour across projections is the discriminating feature.

The decision rule is straightforward: if you see classic teacup layering on the lateral view, assign M2 and reassure. If the lateral view shows *no* change in morphology, or if *any* pleomorphic or fine linear branching calcifications are present, treat as suspicious.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M2** (after magnification and lateral confirm layering), **U1–U2**
- Reassurance; return to routine recall.
- If pleomorphic or linear branching elements are identified in the cluster: upgrade to **M4–M5**; proceed to stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- The true lateral or ML view is *mandatory* to evaluate calcifications for layering — do not rely on CC alone.
- In dense breasts, small teacups can be obscured; increase kVp and use fine-detail magnification.
- Mixed clusters occur: milk of calcium and DCIS can coexist. Always target the *most morphologically suspicious* component for biopsy; do not let the presence of benign layering calcifications distract from an abnormal subset.

### Key learning points

- Projectional shape change is the single most important criterion for diagnosing milk of calcium.
- Distribution modulates suspicion but does not override morphological behaviour across projections.

## Case 12.4: Architectural distortion — radial scar vs carcinoma

### Clinical context
Fifty-eight-year-old woman, screen recall for subtle distortion, right upper inner quadrant.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Subtle central radiolucency with fine radiating lines and focal distortion; no mass; no calcifications. Best seen on CC; present but faint on MLO.

**DBT:** "Black star" appearance — radiolucent centre with long straight spicules without a mass core. No discrete lesion to measure.

**Ultrasound:** Equivocal — vague posterior shadowing without a convincing mass; normal ducts; no vascular focus.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Architectural distortion without a mass is one of the more demanding findings in breast imaging. The two most important causes are **radial scar/CSL** and **invasive carcinoma** (particularly invasive lobular carcinoma or tubular carcinoma).

Radial scars arise from elastotic fibrosis entrapping ducts in a stellate pattern, producing a radiating distortion *without forming a new mass*. Imaging pointers that favour a radial scar over carcinoma:
- Central radiolucency (entrapped fat)
- Thin, long, straight spicules
- No dominant mass centre on DBT
- Sonographically occult or only vague shadowing

Features more typical of carcinoma:
- Dense, opaque centre
- Shorter, thicker spicules
- Associated calcifications
- Internal vascularity on Doppler

However — and this is the critical teaching point — **imaging overlap is substantial and cannot be reliably resolved without tissue**. Up to a significant minority of radial scars harbour associated atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), lobular neoplasia, or even an associated carcinoma within or adjacent to the lesion. Tissue diagnosis is therefore mandatory for all architectural distortion.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M4, U3**
- Preferred biopsy: **stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB (8–10G)** to sample adequately and reduce underestimation of an associated high-risk or malignant lesion. If ultrasound can convincingly target the lesion, 14G core is a reasonable first step — but a benign or B3 core result that is discordant with M4 imaging must proceed to VAB or surgical excision.

### Pathology

- **B3** complex sclerosing lesion without atypia.
- Management options (which vary between units): vacuum-assisted excision (VAE) to remove the radiological target with clip placement and post-procedure mammogram confirming removal, followed by imaging surveillance; or surgical excision if atypia (ADH, lobular neoplasia) is present, or per local protocol.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Do not dismiss distortion because ultrasound is negative — ILC, tubular carcinoma, and radial scars are all frequently sonographically occult. The mammographic/DBT finding is the primary driver of management here.
- Confirm adequate targeting by reviewing the post-biopsy mammogram: the clip must lie within the radiological distortion.
- Do not confuse an incidental radial scar found at surgical excision for an unrelated indication with a screen-detected lesion requiring its own management pathway.

### Key learning points

- Architectural distortion without a mass requires tissue diagnosis because the imaging differential cannot be resolved non-invasively.
- DBT-guided VAB is the preferred biopsy method for pure distortion — it improves diagnostic accuracy and may be therapeutic for B3 lesions without atypia.

## Case 12.5: Ill-defined increased density — invasive lobular carcinoma

### Clinical context
Sixty-four-year-old woman, symptomatic clinic: vague thickening, left upper outer quadrant. Clinical score: P3 (indeterminate thickening).

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Subtle asymmetric density and architectural distortion without a discrete mass; slight nipple retraction. Scattered punctate calcifications not clearly associated with the distortion. Breast density: scattered to heterogeneously dense.

**DBT:** Better delineation of the distortion and subtle linear spicules along Cooper's ligaments; no clear mass centre.

**Ultrasound:** Ill-defined hypoechoic area with posterior shadowing and poor margins over approximately 25 mm — not a circumscribed mass. Normal axillary nodes.

**MRI** (indicated for extent assessment given discordant, ill-defined conventional imaging findings): Regional clumped non-mass enhancement with segmental distribution; persistent to plateau kinetics; extent approximately 38 mm. Possible additional contralateral focus measuring 6 mm.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is the archetypal subtle breast cancer. Its growth pattern — single-file infiltration along stromal planes with minimal desmoplasia — means it rarely forms a discrete mass. Instead it produces thickening, asymmetric density, and architectural distortion. This also makes it **prone to both mammographic and sonographic underestimation** of extent.

Differentials include radial scar/CSL, focal fibrosis, and post-inflammatory scarring. MRI non-mass enhancement with segmental distribution and plateau kinetics is suspicious for malignancy (including DCIS), but background parenchymal enhancement (particularly in HRT users) can confound interpretation.

The principle to internalise: **in the symptomatic context, clinical suspicion of P3 combined with M4/U4 imaging is sufficient to mandate biopsy regardless of what any single modality shows**. Triple assessment concordance drives the decision.

### UK scoring and management

- **P3, M4, U4**
- Biopsy: ultrasound-guided cores from the hypoechoic region; clip placed. If ultrasound-guided sampling yields non-diagnostic tissue and the DBT target remains visible, consider DBT-guided VAB. The contralateral MRI focus should be assessed — if biopsy would alter management (e.g., move from breast-conserving surgery to bilateral mastectomy), MRI-guided biopsy is appropriate.

### Pathology

- **B5b** classic invasive lobular carcinoma, grade 2; ER positive, HER2 negative.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- A normal or near-normal ultrasound should *not* reassure you when clinical and mammographic/DBT suspicion is high. ILC is sonographically subtle by nature.
- MRI usefully extends extent estimation but can both overestimate and underestimate — always interpret MRI findings alongside conventional imaging and clinical context, not in isolation.
- In HRT users, significant background parenchymal enhancement can make MRI interpretation difficult; clinical context and prior imaging comparison are essential.

### Key learning points

- ILC produces characteristically subtle imaging signs because of its infiltrative, non-desmoplastic growth pattern; maintain a low biopsy threshold when clinical features and distortion or asymmetry coexist.
- MRI adds value for extent assessment in ILC and should be considered when conventional imaging underestimates disease.

## Case 12.6: Pleomorphic microcalcifications — high-grade DCIS

### Clinical context
Sixty-two-year-old woman, screen recall for new calcifications, right upper outer quadrant.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Clustered pleomorphic and fine linear branching calcifications, segmental distribution over approximately 45 mm. Background density is not limiting here.

**Magnification views:** Heterogeneous pleomorphism with fine linear and branching forms consistent with casts of necrotic ducts.

**Ultrasound:** Often negative for pure DCIS without a mass component; here, mild ductal ectasia with echogenic foci but no discrete mass.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Fine linear and branching calcifications reflect calcium depositing within necrotic ductal secretions — the so-called **cast pattern** of DCIS with comedonecrosis. The key mechanistic principle: high-grade DCIS produces extensive central necrosis, and calcium precipitates within necrotic material in the duct lumen, creating linear casts that follow the ductal anatomy. **Segmental distribution** therefore traces the ductal tree, reflecting spread along a duct system towards the nipple.

Differentials for pleomorphic microcalcifications to keep in mind:
- **Benign secretory calcifications**: rod-like, coarse, bilateral, typically subareolar — very different morphology
- **Sclerosing adenosis**: amorphous calcifications, often regional
- **Fat necrosis**: eggshell or rim calcification, with clinical context
- **Lobular neoplasia**: can occasionally produce amorphous calcifications

The morphology here (pleomorphic + fine linear branching) and segmental distribution are strongly malignant — M5.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M5**
- Biopsy: **stereotactic or DBT-guided VAB**, targeting the most suspicious morphological area. Aim for at least 12 cores; request specimen radiography to confirm calcifications are present within retrieved cores. Place a clip.
- Preoperative localisation will need to encompass the full extent of calcification — wire localisation, radioactive seed, or radar reflector depending on local practice.

### Pathology

- **B5a** high-grade DCIS with comedonecrosis; ER negative.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Sample the area of worst morphology — this gives the highest probability of upstaging (finding invasive disease) and provides accurate grade for surgical planning.
- If a mass is present or ultrasound identifies a discrete lesion, biopsy that separately as well as the calcifications; an invasive focus may coexist.
- At surgery, specimen radiography must confirm complete excision of the calcified extent — this is not optional.

### Key learning points

- Calcification morphology directly reflects underlying ductal pathology; the cast pattern of fine linear branching calcifications is a malignant hallmark.
- VAB improves sampling yield for calcifications compared with 14G core; specimen radiography closes the quality loop.

## Case 12.7: Circumscribed oval mass — fibroadenoma

### Clinical context
Twenty-three-year-old woman, symptomatic clinic, palpable mobile lump, right 3 o'clock. Clinical score: P2 (clinically benign).

### Imaging findings

**Ultrasound** (first-line in women under 40): 18 mm ovoid, parallel, homogeneously hypoechoic mass with circumscribed margins, thin echogenic pseudocapsule, gentle lobulations, mild posterior enhancement, minimal internal vascularity. No suspicious features.

**Mammography:** Not routinely required in women under 40 with classic sonographic appearances. If performed: well-circumscribed isodense mass — but in this age group adds radiation without diagnostic gain when ultrasound is typical.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Classic fibroadenoma characteristics on ultrasound arise from the combination of cellular stromal and epithelial components in a well-defined lobule: the pseudocapsule reflects the compressed adjacent breast tissue; parallel orientation indicates the lesion is growing along tissue planes rather than across them. These features carry a high positive predictive value for benignity in a young woman with a clinically mobile lump.

Differentials to consider:
- **Phyllodes tumour**: often larger, lobulated with cleft-like cystic spaces, heterogeneous, and may grow rapidly — warrants biopsy
- **Circumscribed carcinoma** (e.g., mucinous or medullary): more common in older women; may show irregular lobulations or angular margins on careful inspection
- **Complicated cyst**: anechoic with debris, no internal flow, mobile internal content

### UK scoring and management

- **P2, U2**
- Whether to biopsy a first-time fibroadenoma in a young woman is genuinely a matter of local policy and shared decision-making. Many units discharge women under 25 with concordant clinical and sonographic features without biopsy; many extend this to under 30 or even 35 for classic appearances. For women aged 25–35, practice varies — some units routinely core all solid masses; others use watchful waiting with a follow-up ultrasound at 6–12 months. There is no single right answer; what matters is that the decision is transparent, documented, and discussed with the patient.
- If biopsied: **B2** fibroadenoma.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- "Atypical" fibroadenomas — with calcification, non-parallel orientation, posterior shadowing, or heterogeneous echotexture — warrant biopsy regardless of age.
- Size >3–4 cm or rapid growth raises suspicion for phyllodes; core biopsy is indicated, though distinguishing phyllodes from cellular fibroadenoma histologically can be difficult and requires correlation with imaging and clinical context.

### Key learning points

- Age, clinical feel, and sonographic morphology together support a benign diagnosis without over-investigation.
- Triple assessment concordance (P2 + U2 + B2 if biopsied) underpins safe discharge.

## Case 12.8: Complicated cyst vs solid mass — aspirate or biopsy?

### Clinical context
Forty-six-year-old woman, cyclical mastalgia and new tender lump, upper outer left breast.

### Imaging findings

**Ultrasound:** 12 mm oval lesion — mostly anechoic with low-level internal echoes and a fluid–debris level. Thin wall, no internal Doppler flow, posterior enhancement. Position of internal debris changes with patient repositioning.

**Mammography** (optional): If performed, a round low-density mass; in dense breasts it may be entirely hidden.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

**Complicated cysts** contain proteinaceous debris, old haemorrhage, or inspissated secretions within an otherwise benign cyst wall. The internal echoes represent mobile debris rather than solid tissue: lack of Doppler flow and change in position with repositioning confirm the cystic nature. Posterior enhancement reflects through-transmission of sound through fluid.

The key decision point is whether any **solid component** is present:
- Intracystic mass or mural nodule with internal vascularity → biopsy mandatory (papilloma, intracystic carcinoma)
- Uniform internal echoes or fluid–debris level with no flow → complicated cyst

Clinical context matters too: cyclical mastalgia in a premenopausal woman fits the biology of a complicated cyst responding to hormonal stimulation.

### UK scoring and management

- **P2, U2**
- Management options:
  - If the patient is reassured and the cyst is asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic: observe; repeat ultrasound in 6–12 months if any uncertainty.
  - If tender: needle aspiration under ultrasound guidance for symptom relief. Routine cytology of straw-coloured or green-tinged fluid is not necessary. Send fluid for cytology only if it is blood-stained or clinically atypical.
  - If blood-stained aspirate or any solid mural component: 14G core or VAB to obtain histology.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Always sweep the entire cyst wall with harmonics and repositioning — do not accept a single plane view. A mural nodule can be missed if the wall is not fully inspected.
- Blood-stained aspirate must trigger further evaluation — core biopsy of any residual wall/nodule, or MRI if the cyst collapses fully.
- Do not aspirate a simple cyst that is asymptomatic — aspiration alone does not reduce recurrence and may cause haemorrhage and make the next visit more complex.

### Key learning points

- Ultrasound reliably distinguishes complicated cysts from solid lesions when the full wall is examined and Doppler is used.
- Tailor intervention to symptoms and to the presence or absence of solid components rather than applying a blanket policy of aspiration.

## Case 12.9: Male breast lump — gynaecomastia vs carcinoma

### Clinical context
Sixty-nine-year-old man, unilateral left retroareolar lump with tenderness; currently prescribed spironolactone (an anti-androgen and aldosterone antagonist known to cause gynaecomastia).

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Triangular retroareolar flame-shaped density extending posteriorly — dendritic pattern gynaecomastia. No discrete mass; no microcalcifications.

**Ultrasound:** Hypoechoic subareolar disc-like tissue with radiating fibrous bands; no focal mass; mildly increased vascularity.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Gynaecomastia results from an imbalance between oestrogen and androgen effects on breast tissue, causing ductal and stromal proliferation. The imaging pattern reflects its stage:
- **Nodular/florid** (early, active): fan-shaped or triangular subareolar density, central, tender — the pattern seen here
- **Dendritic** (later, fibrous): branching or triangular density with fibrous trabeculae, less tender, more established

Male breast carcinoma, by contrast, is typically **eccentric to the nipple**, forms an irregular mass, may have associated microcalcifications or skin/nipple retraction, and does not have a dendritic pattern. Risk factors include Klinefelter's syndrome and *BRCA2* pathogenic variant.

Spironolactone is a recognised cause of gynaecomastia — identifying a causative medication is clinically useful and may inform management.

### UK scoring and management

- **P2, M2, U2**
- Discuss with the prescribing team whether spironolactone can be substituted. Reassure the patient; no follow-up imaging required if appearances are classic and concordant.
- Reserve biopsy for any eccentric, irregular, or non-classic features.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Unilateral gynaecomastia is common and does not require bilaterality to be benign — the imaging pattern, not the laterality, determines the diagnosis.
- Pseudogynaecomastia (fatty enlargement without glandular proliferation) in obese men has a purely fatty appearance on mammography and ultrasound; distinguish from true gynaecomastia, which has glandular/fibrous tissue.

### Key learning points

- Recognise the classic imaging patterns of gynaecomastia to avoid unnecessary biopsy.
- A palpable retroareolar "lump" in a man is usually gynaecomastia, but eccentric location, irregular margins, or microcalcifications mandate biopsy.

## Case 12.10: Fat necrosis — the great mimicker

### Clinical context
Forty-eight-year-old woman, palpable firm lump at site of a seatbelt injury six months previously.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Oil cyst with rim calcification; mixed radiolucent–dense mass with thin eggshell calcification; surrounding mild architectural distortion. (In an earlier post-injury stage, fat necrosis may appear as a spiculated mass-like density without lucency or calcification.)

**Ultrasound:** Complex lesion — echogenic internal bands, posterior shadowing, no internal vascularity. Appearances vary with the stage and extent of fat necrosis.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Fat necrosis occurs when adipose tissue is damaged (trauma, surgery, radiotherapy, infection). The imaging appearances change predictably with time, and understanding this evolution prevents both under- and over-diagnosis:

1. **Acute/early** (days–weeks): haemorrhage, oedema → ill-defined density or spiculated mass-like area; can be indistinguishable from carcinoma on imaging alone
2. **Intermediate** (weeks–months): central liquefaction → oil cyst; lucent centre developing
3. **Late** (months–years): calcium deposits around the cyst wall → eggshell or rim calcification; this is *pathognomonic* for fat necrosis

In the late phase, a classic oil cyst with rim calcification is M2 and requires no biopsy. In the early phase, biopsy may be necessary to exclude carcinoma — clinical history supports the diagnosis but does not replace it.

### UK scoring and management

- **P3** (palpable firm lump); **M2–M4** depending on stage; **U2–U3** if indeterminate
- Classic oil cyst with eggshell calcification: M2, U2, reassure.
- Early or indeterminate spiculated mass: biopsy to exclude carcinoma even in the presence of a relevant history — trauma or prior surgery does not preclude a synchronous cancer.
- If biopsied: **B2** fat necrosis.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Early fat necrosis is radiologically indistinguishable from invasive carcinoma; do not allow clinical history alone to prevent biopsy of an indeterminate lesion.
- Post-surgical fat necrosis at or near a scar is common; always place a radio-opaque skin marker at incision sites before mammography.
- MRI may show rim enhancement of an oil cyst — this does not indicate malignancy, but awareness prevents misinterpretation.

### Key learning points

- Understand the temporal evolution of fat necrosis: the stage determines the imaging appearance and therefore the management.
- Classic late-stage appearances are diagnostic; early appearances require biopsy for concordance.

## Case 12.11: Axillary nodal abnormality on screening mammography

### Clinical context
Fifty-seven-year-old woman, routine screening; asymmetric density noted in the left axillary tail on the MLO view.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Left axillary tail: one enlarged lymph node with cortical thickening to 5 mm, lobulated cortex, reduced visible fatty hilum.

**Ultrasound:** Confirms an enlarged node with focal eccentric cortical bulge; cortical thickness 5.5 mm; compressed hilum; increased peripheral vascularity on Doppler.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Normal lymph nodes have a thin, uniform cortex (typically <3 mm) and a prominent fatty hilum. Malignant infiltration typically produces:
- **Focal** (eccentric) cortical thickening — the most specific sign
- Loss or compression of the fatty hilum
- Altered vascularity (peripheral or absent hilar flow)

Differentials include reactive hyperplasia (diffuse, uniform, thin cortical thickening with preserved hilum), dermatopathic nodes (can cause cortical thickening in skin conditions), and granulomatous disease. An isolated abnormal node in an asymptomatic screening participant — *especially with focal eccentric thickening and hilum loss* — is suspicious for metastatic disease, either from an occult ipsilateral breast primary or from another source (e.g., melanoma, lymphoma).

Note: Vaccination-related adenopathy (e.g., post-COVID-19 vaccination) is a recognised cause of transient axillary nodal enlargement; timing of vaccination relative to imaging is clinically relevant.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M3, U4**
- Ultrasound-guided needle biopsy of the most abnormal node. Practice varies: 14G core is preferred in many units for its sensitivity (including for micrometastases); FNA is used in some centres as a first-line approach. Clip the sampled node for subsequent surgical planning.
- Thorough ipsilateral breast assessment with DBT and ultrasound for an occult primary; if no primary identified and nodal metastasis is confirmed, **MRI** is indicated.

### Pathology

- **B5b** metastatic carcinoma; immunohistochemistry consistent with breast primary.
- MRI identifies an 8 mm enhancing focus in the upper outer breast; MRI-guided biopsy **B5b** confirms the occult invasive primary.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- The axillae should be systematically included when reviewing MLO views in both screening and assessment contexts.
- Nodal morphology is more important than absolute size: a small node with focal cortical thickening is more suspicious than a large node with a uniform thin cortex and preserved hilum.
- Do not overlook vaccine-related adenopathy, but do not use it as a reason to dismiss genuinely suspicious morphology — correlation with vaccination history and follow-up if timing is relevant.

### Key learning points

- Focal eccentric cortical thickening with hilum loss is the key malignant nodal signature; morphology trumps size.
- An abnormal axillary node may be the only sign of an occult breast primary; thorough breast search and MRI are indicated when nodal metastasis is confirmed without an obvious primary.

## Case 12.12: Paget's disease of the nipple

### Clinical context
Sixty-six-year-old woman, eczematous nipple change and bloody discharge not responding to topical treatment.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Subareolar pleomorphic calcifications with linear forms; nipple retraction; possible subareolar mass.

**Ultrasound:** Dilated retroareolar ducts with echogenic intraductal material; 12 mm hypoechoic subareolar mass adjacent to ducts.

**MRI** (for extent mapping): Linear segmental non-mass enhancement extending retroareolarly over approximately 35 mm, with suspicious kinetics.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Paget's disease arises from intraepidermal spread of malignant cells — typically from an underlying high-grade DCIS or invasive carcinoma — into the nipple epidermis. The clinical hallmark is **persistent unilateral eczematous change unresponsive to topical therapy**, with or without discharge.

The critical point: a normal mammogram does *not* exclude Paget's disease. Up to 50% of cases have no identifiable abnormality on conventional mammography. MRI is much more sensitive and should be used for extent mapping once the diagnosis is suspected.

Differentials:
- **Eczema/dermatitis**: typically bilateral or responds to emollients; does not show subareolar calcifications or mass

### UK scoring and management

- **P4–P5, M4–M5, U4–U5**

  - **Ultrasound-guided core** of any mass
- MDT planning: central excision or mastectomy depending on MRI extent; sentinel node biopsy if invasive component is identified or suspected.

### Pathology

- **B5a** (DCIS with Paget's disease) or **B5b** (with invasive component) depending on biopsy results.

- Do not treat unilateral persistent nipple change as eczema without imaging and biopsy if it fails to respond to a short trial of topical therapy.

## Case 12.13: Lactating patient — mastitis, abscess, or cancer?

### Clinical context
Thirty-three-year-old woman, three months postpartum, erythema and painful lump, upper outer right breast; low-grade fever; antibiotics commenced by GP.

**Ultrasound** (first-line in this context): Diffuse subcutaneous oedema, increased echogenicity, skin thickening, hyperaemia. Focal 22 mm complex fluid collection with peripheral hyperaemia and internal debris — consistent with an abscess.

**Mammography:** Generally avoided in lactation because of limited sensitivity (dense lactating parenchyma) and patient discomfort; used only if there is diagnostic uncertainty about a non-inflammatory component.

- Prominent axillary lymphadenopathy
- Non-lactating woman

### UK scoring and management

## Case 12.14: Post-operative breast — scar vs recurrence

### Clinical context
Sixty-two-year-old woman, three years post–breast-conserving surgery and radiotherapy for left invasive ductal carcinoma; new distortion near the lumpectomy scar on surveillance mammography.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Focal architectural distortion at the lumpectomy site; surgical clips in situ; increased density from fibrosis consistent with post-treatment change.

**DBT:** Linear spiculations converging to the scar; no discrete nodular mass. *Stable compared with the prior year's mammogram.*

**Ultrasound:** Hypoechoic linear scar tissue without a focal mass; no internal vascularity.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Post-surgical and post-radiotherapy change peaks in the first one to two years and then gradually stabilises. **Temporal change is the single most powerful discriminator between scar and recurrence**:
- Stable or *decreasing* distortion/density: almost always scar
- *New or progressive* mass, increasing distortion, or new pleomorphic calcifications at or near the scar: suspicious for recurrence

Features on a single examination that raise concern for recurrence:
- A nodular mass component (scar tends to be linear, retracted along the incision axis)
- New microcalcifications, especially pleomorphic
- Eccentric location relative to the expected scar line
- Internal vascularity on Doppler ultrasound

Differentials: fat necrosis (evolves, may be initially mass-like), seroma (fluid-containing, thin wall), suture granuloma, and true recurrence.

### UK scoring and management

- Stable findings: **M2–M3, U2** → continue surveillance.
- New or progressive findings: **M4–M5 / U4–U5** → biopsy targeted to any mass or the most suspicious area; stereotactic or DBT-guided biopsy for new calcifications.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Always compare with the immediate post-operative baseline film and every subsequent surveillance mammogram — a single study in isolation cannot reveal the trend.
- Scar tends to *retract inwards along the incision line*; recurrence is often *eccentric, nodular, and grows outwards*.
- After radiotherapy, the ipsilateral breast density increases initially and then usually decreases over two to four years; awareness of this pattern prevents misinterpretation.

### Key learning points

- Temporal trend is the dominant criterion: obtain and review all prior imaging before forming a view.
- DBT separates tethered scar from a new nodular mass effectively.

## Case 12.15: Intramammary lymph node vs small mass

### Clinical context
Fifty-five-year-old woman, screen recall for a 7 mm oval mass, upper outer left breast.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Small oval circumscribed mass with central lucency (fatty hilum) and thin cortex; upper outer quadrant, peripheral location. Stable compared with three years ago (marginally larger by 1–2 mm, within measurement variability).

**Ultrasound:** Oval hypoechoic node with echogenic hilum; cortical thickness 2 mm uniform; hilar vascularity on Doppler.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

The **intramammary lymph node (IMLN)** is a normal anatomical variant — these nodes drain the lateral breast and upper outer quadrant and lie within the breast parenchyma rather than the axilla. Classic features: oval, small (<1 cm), peripheral location (upper outer quadrant), **fatty hilum** on mammography, thin uniform cortex, and hilar flow on Doppler. These features together are pathognomonic.

Differentials:
- **Small fibroadenoma**: no fatty hilum, uniform hypoechoic echotexture, no hilar vascularity
- **Circumscribed metastasis** (e.g., from melanoma or lymphoma): loss of hilum, eccentric cortical thickening
- **Fat necrosis** with central fat: different context, wall irregularity, no hilar flow

The fatty hilum is the key distinguishing sign and should be actively sought.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M2, U2**
- Routine recall; no biopsy required when features are classic and stable. Monitor for cortical thickening or hilum loss on subsequent screens — either would warrant re-evaluation.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- IMLNs can enlarge modestly with intercurrent infection or inflammation — interpret any change in clinical context before escalating.
- Do not mistake the hilar fat on mammography for a concerning lucency; the oval, thin-walled, peripheral morphology distinguishes it from an oil cyst.

### Key learning points

- The fatty hilum is the diagnostic sign for IMLN; identifying it prevents unnecessary biopsy.
- Awareness of the normal peripheral upper outer quadrant location of IMLNs is as important as recognising the morphological features.

## Case 12.16: Tomosynthesis reduces recall — summation vs true distortion

### Clinical context
Fifty-nine-year-old woman, routine screening. First reader recalls for possible distortion on the right MLO; second reader disagrees; arbitration recommends assessment with DBT.

### Imaging findings

**2D mammography:** Subtle distortion suggested in mid posterior breast on MLO.

**DBT:** Sequential slices through the area reveal crossing Cooper's ligaments and vessels at different depths; no true convergence; "distortion" disappears when scrolling through adjacent slices.

**Ultrasound:** No correlating focal lesion.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

DBT works by acquiring images from multiple angles and reconstructing 1 mm slices through the breast, separating structures at different depths. This removes the tissue overlap that creates summation artefact on standard 2D mammography.

**True architectural distortion** on DBT:
- Persists across multiple adjacent slices at the same depth
- Shows converging lines or a central point of retraction
- May have a mass or distortion centre

**Summation artefact** on DBT:
- Disappears as slices advance (structures are at different depths)
- No convergence when adjacent slices are viewed
- No correlating ultrasound finding

The lesson here is not to read DBT in isolation on a single slice — the hallmark of a true lesion is *persistence across slices*, not its appearance on any one slice.

### UK scoring and management

- **P1, M2** (after DBT), **U1**
- Reassurance; return to routine recall.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Never interpret DBT by freezing on a single suspicious-looking slice; scroll through multiple adjacent slices to assess persistence.
- Synthetic 2D images generated from DBT data can occasionally introduce their own artefacts; correlate with the DBT slices themselves.
- DBT is increasingly used in assessment clinics across the UK for exactly this purpose — reducing false-positive recalls without missing true lesions.

### Key learning points

- DBT resolves tissue summation by separating anatomical planes; true lesions persist across slices while artefacts do not.
- Systematic scrolling technique is as important as image quality.

## Case 12.17: Papillary lesion — intraductal mass with vascular stalk

### Clinical context
Forty-seven-year-old woman, unilateral serosanguinous nipple discharge.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Often normal; may show dilated retroareolar ducts. No calcifications in this case.

**Ultrasound:** Dilated duct containing an 8 mm intraluminal mass with a feeding vascular stalk on colour Doppler; mobile with gentle probe pressure; no posterior shadowing.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Intraductal papilloma is a benign papillary proliferation arising from ductal epithelium; it has a fibrovascular core that produces the visible Doppler signal. The combination of dilated duct, intraluminal mass, feeding vascular stalk, and mobility is highly characteristic.

Differentials:
- **Intraductal debris or clot**: avascular on Doppler; may change position or appearance but lacks a stalk
- **DCIS**: intraductal echogenic material, often with associated calcifications, no discrete vascular stalk
- **Papillary carcinoma**: similar morphology but often more irregular, may be peripheral in location, may have associated DCIS; imaging alone cannot reliably distinguish from benign papilloma

The key principle: imaging cannot reliably exclude atypia or malignancy within a papillary lesion — **tissue diagnosis is always required**.

### UK scoring and management

- **P3** (discharge), **U3–U4**
- Image-guided biopsy: many centres favour **VAB** over 14G core because papillomas are prone to sampling error (the fibrovascular stalk may be missed on a single core, and atypia may be focal). VAB reduces underestimation and may excise small papillomas therapeutically.
- Cytology of discharge is not diagnostically reliable — its role is limited.

### Pathology

- **B3** intraductal papilloma without atypia.
- Management: VAE with imaging confirmation of complete removal, followed by clinical and imaging surveillance. If atypia is present (B3 with atypia) or lesions are multiple/peripheral, surgical excision is generally recommended because of the higher upgrade risk.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Peripheral papillary lesions carry a higher risk of upgrade to atypia or malignancy than central (retroareolar) papillomas; lower the threshold for surgical excision.
- Optimise Doppler settings (low pulse repetition frequency, low wall filter) for intraductal flow — it can be missed with standard settings.

### Key learning points

- The vascular stalk on Doppler is the characteristic sign; VAB improves diagnostic accuracy and may be therapeutic for small central papillomas.
- Tissue diagnosis is always required for papillary lesions regardless of how typical the imaging appears.

## Case 12.18: Breast implants — capsular contracture vs rupture

### Clinical context
Forty-five-year-old woman with cosmetic silicone implants; new firmness and shape change affecting the right breast.

### Imaging findings

**Mammography:** Limited utility for parenchymal assessment; **Eklund (implant-displaced) views** used to push implant posteriorly and visualise the native breast tissue. Implant shell edges appear intact in this case.

**Ultrasound:** Right implant: echogenic "stepladder" sign — multiple parallel hyperechoic lines within the implant lumen, representing the collapsed intracapsular shell. No snowstorm artefact in the periprosthetic or breast tissue (excludes extracapsular silicone).

**MRI** (if surgery is planned or ultrasound is equivocal): **Linguine sign** — multiple curvilinear low-signal lines within the implant on T2-weighted or STIR silicone-selective sequences, representing the folded intracapsular shell. No extracapsular silicone identified.

### Reasoning and differential diagnosis

Silicone implant rupture is classified by the integrity of the fibrous capsule:

- **Intracapsular rupture**: silicone shell is breached but contained within the fibrous capsule. Stepladder sign on ultrasound; linguine sign on MRI. Patient may notice firmness or shape change.
- **Extracapsular rupture**: silicone leaks beyond the fibrous capsule into breast tissue or axilla. Snowstorm artefact on ultrasound (diffuse echogenic foci in tissue from free silicone); high T2 signal silicone outside the capsule on MRI.
- **Capsular contracture**: the fibrous capsule thickens and contracts *without* shell rupture; implant deformity but no intracapsular signs of rupture on ultrasound or MRI.

### UK scoring and management

- **P2, U2–U3**; MRI for confirmation if surgery is planned.
- Referral to plastic surgery for management; this is not a breast cancer pathway unless a separate breast lesion is identified.

### Pitfalls and pearls

- Mammography with implants substantially reduces parenchymal sensitivity; always use Eklund views and document technique.
- Silicone granulomas (siliconomas) from prior extracapsular rupture can form mass-like lesions in breast tissue or axilla; correlation with implant history prevents misdiagnosis.
- Radial folds (normal infolding of the implant shell) can be mistaken for rupture on ultrasound — they are single, smooth, linear, and do not produce the stepladder pattern.

### Key learning points

- Recognise the stepladder (ultrasound) and linguine (MRI) signs for intracapsular rupture; MRI is the gold standard for confirmation when the result will influence surgical planning.
- Eklund views are mandatory technique in implant patients, not an optional extra.

## Cross-cutting themes and teaching points

### Anatomy underpins appearances

Understanding normal breast anatomy makes abnormalities predictable rather than arbitrary:
- **Spiculation** follows Cooper's ligaments; desmoplasia retracts them.
- **Segmental calcifications** trace the ductal tree towards the nipple.
- **Architectural distortion without mass** arises when a process (radial scar, ILC) disrupts the fibrous stroma without creating a new tissue bulk.
- **IMLNs** sit peripheral and superolateral, tracking axillary drainage.
- **Subareolar lesions** (Paget's, papilloma, DCIS) map to the nipple–areolar complex.

### The UK pathway is a system, not a checklist

- In **screening**, double reading and arbitration exist to balance sensitivity and specificity across a population.
- In **assessment and symptomatic clinics**, triple assessment is a framework for combining complementary information — its power lies in using *concordance* to reach safe decisions. When the P, M, U, and B scores point in the same direction, confidence in the conclusion is high. When they diverge, the discordance itself is diagnostic information demanding action: re-biopsy, additional imaging, or MDT discussion.
- **B categories** guide post-biopsy management:
  - B1: normal tissue or insufficient material — repeat or re-biopsy if discordant with imaging
  - B2: benign — concordant with benign imaging; discharge or routine follow-up
  - B3: lesion of uncertain malignant potential (e.g., papilloma, radial scar, flat epithelial atypia, lobular neoplasia) — management varies by lesion type, atypia, and local protocol; typically VAE or surgical excision
  - B4: suspicious — excision
  - B5a: in situ malignancy (DCIS); B5b: invasive malignancy — oncological treatment pathway

### Modality strengths and limitations

| Modality | Excels at | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Mammography | Calcifications, distortion, screening | Dense breasts, ILC, postoperative change |
| DBT | Resolving summation, delineating masses, distortion | Radiation dose (modest), calcification display varies by software |
| Ultrasound | Mass characterisation, biopsy guidance, axilla, cysts | Operator-dependent, DCIS without mass often occult |
| MRI | Extent assessment, occult primary, implant integrity, response to neoadjuvant therapy | Cost, availability, background parenchymal enhancement, false positives |

**Use MRI selectively** — indiscriminate use generates false positives, delays treatment, and increases mastectomy rates without always improving outcomes. Define the clinical question before requesting it.

### Negative imaging does not trump clinical suspicion

This principle cannot be overstated. A persistent, firm, irregular palpable abnormality in a woman of appropriate age requires biopsy even if mammography and ultrasound are reported as normal. Sensitivity of any single modality is not 100%. The symptomatic pathway exists for this reason.

### Technique is not optional

Problem-solving views (spot compression, rolled views, true lateral for calcifications, magnification), Eklund views in implant patients, and axillary scanning in assessment are core competencies. They change management. Ensure they are performed, not assumed.

### Biopsy results must be correlated — always

- Report the imaging target with precision.
- Review specimen radiography (for calcification-targeted biopsies).
- Check the post-biopsy mammogram confirms clip position within the lesion.
- Ask: does the pathology result explain the imaging? If B2 histology does not account for an M5 lesion, the result is discordant, and the lesion should be re-biopsied or surgically excised.

### Practice varies — and that is acceptable

Thresholds for axillary node biopsy, management of B3 lesions, use of DBT in the screening setting, and extent of MRI use all vary between UK units and evolve with evidence. When you are uncertain, involve the MDT. When practice in your unit differs from what you have read, understand the rationale for your local protocol rather than assuming one approach is universally correct.

## Summary

Accurate breast imaging is built on three foundations:

1. **Understanding mechanisms**: how pathological processes reshape normal breast anatomy, and why that produces specific imaging signs. When you understand the mechanism, you can reason from first principles rather than pattern-matching alone.

2. **Applying the UK pathway systematically**: always work within the triple assessment framework; always demand concordance; always act on discordance.

3. **Using modalities intelligently**: know what each modality is good at and where it fails; use problem-solving techniques before escalating to more invasive investigations; select biopsy methods appropriate to the target.

Approach every case with structure, correlate across modalities and with pathology, and — crucially — bring humility to the decision. Breast imaging involves genuine uncertainty; that is why the MDT exists.
