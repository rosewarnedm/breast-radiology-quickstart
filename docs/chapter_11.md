# Chapter 11: Quality Assurance and Safety

## 11.1 Why QA and Safety Matter

Quality assurance is not an administrative afterthought — it is what makes breast imaging clinically reliable. Image quality, positioning, exposure, and interpretation processes directly determine cancer detection rates, false-positive rates, and whether patients are harmed or helped. Poor QA causes missed cancers, unnecessary recalls, avoidable biopsies, and excessive radiation. These failures are measurable, auditable, and largely preventable.

In the UK, QA is mandated rather than optional. The Ionising Radiation (Medical Exposure) Regulations (IR(ME)R) govern justification, optimisation, and clinical evaluation of every medical exposure. The Ionising Radiations Regulations (IRR) protect staff and the public. The NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) sets national standards and key performance indicators (KPIs) for screening services, and professional bodies — the Royal College of Radiologists (RCR), Society of Radiographers (SCoR), Institute of Physics and Engineering in Medicine (IPEM), and British Medical Ultrasound Society (BMUS) — provide overlapping technical and governance guidance.

The goal is not flawlessness but a reliable system that improves continuously. Where evidence is uncertain or local constraints apply, decisions should be transparent, reasoned, and auditable.

## 11.2 The UK Framework

### NHSBSP and Symptomatic Services

The NHSBSP invites women aged 50–70 in England (with some local extension) for two-view mammography every three years. All screens are **double read**, with arbitration of discordant opinions; women with suspicious findings are recalled for assessment. This double-read model is one of the system's most important quality safeguards: it consistently improves cancer detection over single reading alone.

Symptomatic services operate differently. Patients are referred under urgent suspected cancer (two-week wait) pathways and assessed using **triple assessment**: clinical examination (P score), imaging (M and/or U score), and percutaneous biopsy histology (B code). The strength of triple assessment lies in its concordance — when all three components agree, diagnostic confidence is high; when they disagree, the system flags the discordance and escalates.

### Regulatory and Governance Architecture

Understanding who does what matters in practice:

- **IR(ME)R**: Requires justification of each exposure, optimisation of dose, and documented clinical evaluation (the radiology report). Significant accidental or unintended exposures (SAUEs) must be investigated and reported under local Employer's Procedures.
- **IRR**: Requires a Radiation Protection Adviser (RPA) and Medical Physics Expert (MPE) to oversee staff and public protection; rooms must be shielded; personal dosimetry used where indicated.
- **NHSBSP QA**: External QA visits, national KPI benchmarking, interval cancer review, and the **PERFORMS scheme** — a proficiency testing programme for screening readers that provides objective external feedback on individual performance.
- **Professional guidance**: IPEM schedules for mammography equipment testing; BMUS for ultrasound; RCR and SCoR for reporting standards, governance, and training requirements.

## 11.3 Image Quality Assurance: Getting the Images Right

Poor images cause two distinct harms: missed cancers and unnecessary recalls. Good QA prevents both. The underlying principle throughout is that image quality is not accidental — it follows from anatomy-informed technique, appropriate equipment, and systematic review.

### 11.3.1 Mammography (2D and Tomosynthesis)

**Positioning and anatomy**

The aim is to include all the fibroglandular tissue at risk. On the mediolateral oblique (MLO), the pectoralis major should be convex in contour and reach to within accepted tolerances of the posterior nipple line (PNL); the inframammary fold should be open and included. On the craniocaudal (CC), the posterior tissue should be included to within an acceptable PNL difference compared with the MLO. The rationale is straightforward: cancers are distributed throughout the breast, and under-represented posterior, inferior, or medial tissue directly correlates with false negatives and interval cancers.

**Compression**

Compression serves multiple purposes simultaneously: it spreads overlapping tissue to improve conspicuity, reduces breast thickness to lower dose, limits motion blur, and stabilises the breast for consistent AEC performance. The correct degree of compression is the minimum needed to achieve stable, even thickness and optimal contrast-to-noise whilst remaining tolerable. This is not a fixed number — it varies with breast size, tissue composition, and patient factors. Patient-centred coaching frequently enables better compression with less perceived pain. Radiographers play a central role here; radiologists should understand why sub-optimal compression degrades the image they are reading.

**Exposure optimisation**

Modern automatic exposure control (AEC) selects kV, target/filter combination, and mAs to balance image contrast and noise for a given breast thickness and composition. Microcalcifications require high spatial resolution and benefit from lower kV; dense breasts may benefit from modified filtration. Dose is quantified as **average glandular dose (AGD)** per view and must be monitored against local diagnostic reference levels (DRLs) and NHSBSP/IPEM performance limits. Tomosynthesis (DBT) and contrast-enhanced mammography (CEM) increase AGD compared with standard 2D; synthesised 2D images generated from tomosynthesis data can mitigate the cumulative dose when replacing a conventional 2D acquisition.

**PGMI image quality scoring**

In screening, each image is categorised as Perfect, Good, Moderate, or Inadequate-Repeat. PGMI scoring provides structured radiographer feedback on positioning, compression, sharpness, exposure, and artefacts. Reject/repeat analysis at unit level identifies systematic training needs or equipment faults. As a trainee, you should understand these categories well enough to recognise when images you are reading are borderline adequate and to flag this in your report.

**Equipment QA (in partnership with Medical Physics)**

This covers acceptance testing on new or upgraded units, followed by routine constancy testing on daily, weekly, monthly, and annual cycles per NHSBSP/IPEM schedules. Tests include AEC reproducibility, kV/mAs accuracy, detector uniformity, artefact evaluation, low-contrast detectability, spatial resolution (MTF), and AGD checks. After major maintenance or software updates, re-testing is required before returning to clinical use. Equipment failure silent to the radiologist — for example a slow drift in AEC calibration — will degrade images across an entire caseload before it becomes obvious. Routine testing catches these drifts.

**Display devices and reading conditions**

Mammography requires high-specification primary diagnostic monitors calibrated to the DICOM Greyscale Standard Display Function (GSDF), with regular luminance and uniformity checks by physics. Low ambient light in the reading room is not optional — it is a diagnostic requirement, because contrast perception for microcalcifications is directly degraded by glare and bright ambient light. Daily user checks (e.g., SMPTE pattern review) complement periodic physics testing.

**Tomosynthesis-specific considerations**

DBT QA additionally covers angular accuracy, slice sensitivity profile, in-plane resolution, and artefact evaluation. Two artefacts deserve specific mention: **out-of-plane blur** (where a structure from an adjacent slice bleeds through) can occasionally mimic or mask spiculation, and **staircase artefacts** at high-contrast interfaces can be misread as calcification. Familiarity with these prevents over-calling. Locally, units must decide whether to acquire synthesised 2D alone, conventional 2D combined with DBT, or DBT only for assessment cases — balancing dose, workflow, and diagnostic yield. Practice legitimately varies.

**Common positioning pitfalls**

- Inadequate posterior tissue on CC views — the commonest source of missed posterior cancers
- Rolled or laterally displaced nipple obscuring the retro-areolar region
- Skin folds producing a curvilinear density that mimics architectural distortion
- Motion blur softening microcalcification morphology

All are reducible by positioning coaching, appropriate hold techniques, and immediate radiographer review at the time of acquisition.

### 11.3.2 Ultrasound

Ultrasound quality is harder to standardise than mammography because it is operator-dependent in real time. Good QA starts with correct presets: breast-specific settings, appropriate focal zone placement, high-frequency linear transducers, and balanced gain/TGC so that the tissue immediately behind a mass is not artificially suppressed. Scanning should follow ductal anatomy — radial and antiradial planes — and be supplemented by orthogonal views for lesion documentation.

**Routine QA**

Physics testing (in line with BMUS and local schedules) covers probe uniformity, dead element detection, depth calibration, and grey-scale and Doppler performance using appropriate phantoms. Cable and connector integrity should be checked. Reverberation and side-lobe artefacts can mimic calcifications or distort margins; document and act on systematic artefacts promptly.

**Lesion localisation and concordance**

Use consistent anatomical landmarks — clockface position, distance from nipple, transducer orientation — so that the lesion you describe in your report can be reliably found again, matched to a mammographic finding, or targeted for biopsy. Poor localisation documentation is a patient safety issue.

**Common pitfalls**

- Gain set too low hides hypoechoic spiculation at the margin; too high obscures margins and obliterates posterior acoustic features
- A parallel lesion appears antiparallel if the transducer is not truly perpendicular — heel-toe and rocking adjustments are essential before assigning orientation
- Failure to scan radially risks missing small intraductal lesions

### 11.3.3 Breast MRI

Protocol standardisation is non-negotiable for MRI: bilateral coverage with a dedicated breast coil, high-resolution T1 fat-saturated dynamic post-contrast sequences with adequate temporal resolution, plus T2/STIR for oedema and cystic characterisation. Subtracted images and maximum intensity projections (MIPs) aid overview, but must always be interpreted alongside the native source images — subtraction misregistration from patient movement creates pseudo-enhancement that can mimic genuine enhancement. Cover from sternum to latissimus dorsi and from the clavicle to the inframammary fold; incomplete coverage leads to missed axillary tail or IMF lesions.

**QA**

Regular SNR and ghosting checks, geometric accuracy testing, fat suppression uniformity, and coil element performance monitoring are required. After software upgrades or coil changes, re-validation before clinical use is essential — changes in reconstruction algorithms can alter the appearance of enhancement kinetics.

**Common pitfalls**

- Inhomogeneous fat suppression creates areas of apparent enhancement — compare with native T1 and T2 to distinguish real from artefactual enhancement
- Background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) can obscure or mimic non-mass enhancement; where possible, schedule premenopausal women in the second week of their cycle when BPE is lowest
- Cardiac and respiratory motion can produce regional non-mass enhancement patterns

### 11.3.4 Contrast-Enhanced Mammography (CEM)

CEM acquires dual-energy pairs before and after intravenous iodinated contrast, with algorithmic recombination to generate a subtracted enhancement image. QA covers exposure pairing calibration, recombination algorithm performance, and recognition of artefacts — skin folds and biopsy clips produce characteristic "blooming" on the recombined image. Dose is higher than standard 2D mammography; protocols must be audited against DRLs. Safety considerations align with iodinated contrast use generally (see 11.4.4).

### 11.3.5 Specimen Imaging

The primary purpose of specimen imaging is to confirm that the radiological target — whether a lesion, calcifications, or a marker clip — has been retrieved. Orient the specimen as agreed with the surgical team (using sutures or clips). For microcalcification excision, high-resolution imaging is often necessary, and immediate repeat imaging should be performed if the result is inadequate — delaying this risks re-operation. Communication between radiology and surgery must be timely, documented, and explicit.

## 11.4 Radiation Protection and Contrast Safety

### 11.4.1 Core Principles under IR(ME)R

Every exposure requires:

- **Justification**: net clinical benefit must outweigh risk. Consider pregnancy status where relevant.
- **Optimisation**: lowest dose compatible with diagnostic quality; monitor AGD and compare with DRLs.
- **Clinical evaluation**: a timely, documented report with failsafe communication of significant findings.

These are legal duties, not aspirations.

### 11.4.2 Mammography Dose

AGD per view varies with breast thickness, composition, and modality. Modern AEC and filtration achieve low AGD at screening. Systematic over-exposure — for example from mis-calibrated AEC or consistently inadequate compression — increases AGD across the screened population and should be identified by dose audit and corrected. Tomosynthesis and CEM increase per-examination dose; synthesised 2D images can partially offset this if replacing rather than supplementing conventional 2D.

### 11.4.3 Pregnancy and Lactation

Ultrasound is first-line in pregnant and lactating patients. Mammography can be justified in pregnancy when clinically indicated — foetal dose from breast imaging is extremely low. MRI without gadolinium is safe in pregnancy. Gadolinium-based contrast agents are generally avoided in pregnancy unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk; if administered, macrocyclic agents are preferred. Both gadolinium and iodinated contrast agents are excreted in breast milk in negligible quantities; breastfeeding need not be interrupted after either agent under current UK guidance, although some centres still advise a brief interruption for iodinated contrast — discuss with your local team.

### 11.4.4 Iodinated Contrast (CEM) and Gadolinium (MRI)

Before contrast administration, assess:

- Prior contrast reactions — type, severity, and agent
- Asthma or significant atopy (increased risk of contrast reaction)
- Renal function — for gadolinium, check eGFR where indicated; avoid in eGFR <30 ml/min/1.73 m² unless essential and discussed with the patient
- Metformin management for iodinated contrast per local policy

An important misconception to correct: **shellfish or seafood allergy is not a reliable predictor of iodinated contrast reaction** and should not on its own preclude CEM. The shared allergen hypothesis has been largely refuted.

Premedication protocols for high-risk patients, immediately available emergency drugs and resuscitation equipment, and staff trained in anaphylaxis management are mandatory for any service administering contrast agents. Extravasation management protocols must also be in place.

### 11.4.5 SAUEs and Incident Reporting

Significant accidental or unintended exposures — including wrong patient, wrong laterality, and far higher than intended doses — must be investigated under local IR(ME)R Employer's Procedures. Root-cause analysis should focus on systems and human factors rather than individual blame. Shared learning from SAUEs across the team and wider network is a regulatory expectation and a clinical duty.

### 11.4.6 Staff Radiation Safety

Mammography and stereotactic rooms are adequately shielded; staff should not be in the primary beam during exposure. Radioactive seed localisation using iodine-125 seeds requires specific IRR/IR(ME)R governance, staff training, and rigorous inventory control and reconciliation at surgery and pathology. Personal dosimetry is used where indicated by local risk assessment.

## 11.5 Procedural Safety: Biopsy, Localisation and Aftercare

Safe procedures depend on a chain: correct patient and site confirmed, appropriate technique chosen, asepsis maintained, haemostasis achieved, and aftercare communicated. Concordance across the P/M/U/B components is the final safety check on any biopsy result.

### 11.5.1 Consent, Identification and Time-Out

Confirm patient identity, laterality, and planned procedure before every intervention. Use a structured pre-procedure pause — analogous to the WHO surgical checklist — for ultrasound, stereotactic, and MR-guided procedures. Check allergies and current medications. Consent should cover benefits, realistic alternatives, risks (including haematoma, infection, vasovagal episode, and the possibility of needing further sampling), and what the patient can expect during recovery. Document this conversation.

### 11.5.2 Anticoagulants and Antiplatelets

Core needle biopsy generally proceeds on aspirin alone. For clopidogrel, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), or warfarin, decisions are individualised based on the bleeding risk of the planned procedure and the patient's thrombotic risk. Vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB) and stereotactic procedures carry higher bleeding risk than standard core biopsy. Do not stop dual antiplatelet therapy early after recent coronary stenting without specialist input — the thrombotic risk may far exceed the procedural bleeding risk. For VAB, some centres ask patients to omit a DOAC dose or confirm that the INR is within agreed limits for warfarin; practice varies and should be documented explicitly. Liaise with the referrer or anticoagulation service when uncertain.

### 11.5.3 Local Anaesthesia

Lidocaine 1% is standard. Calculate the maximum safe dose before drawing up: approximately 3 mg/kg without adrenaline, higher with adrenaline (check your local formulary). Aspirate before injection to avoid intravascular administration. Buffering with sodium bicarbonate or warming the solution reduces the stinging sensation and is well tolerated by patients. True allergy to lidocaine is rare; document any history and consider mepivacaine or a specialist anaesthetic review if genuine allergy is suspected.

### 11.5.4 Asepsis and Infection Control

Sterile technique is required for all percutaneous biopsies and localisations. Routine antibiotic prophylaxis is not indicated; consider it in selected cases (immunosuppression, prosthetic heart valves) according to local policy. Skin preparation, sterile draping, and careful instrument handling are the primary infection controls.

### 11.5.5 Technique-Specific Considerations

**Ultrasound-guided core biopsy or VAB**

Maintain continuous visualisation of the needle tip throughout the procedure. Use colour Doppler beforehand to identify and avoid vessels, particularly in hypervascular lesions or near the axilla. Chest wall proximity significantly increases pneumothorax risk — reduce this by using a shallow angle and careful depth control. For superficial lesions, ensure sufficient anaesthesia and that the throw of the biopsy device will not breach the chest wall.

**Stereotactic core biopsy or VAB**

Confirm calcifications are present in specimen radiographs before the patient leaves the room. Deploy a marker clip before removing the needle and re-image to confirm clip position relative to the biopsy cavity — clip migration can occur, and documentation at the time prevents future confusion. Manage vasovagal reactions proactively: the prone position and procedural anxiety are significant triggers; warn patients beforehand and monitor throughout.

**MRI-guided biopsy**

This is technically demanding for both patient and operator. Ensure robust MRI safety screening, including active implant assessment. Have a pre-agreed protocol for converting to second-look ultrasound if the target lesion is identifiable sonographically — MRI-only lesions that are not found on second-look ultrasound require MRI guidance, and abandoning the procedure without a clear plan is not acceptable.

**Pre-operative localisation**

- *Wire localisation*: secure the wire to the skin, document its depth and trajectory, and counsel the patient to avoid strenuous shoulder movement. Deep trajectories towards the chest wall carry a small risk of pleural breach — plan the entry point and angle to avoid aiming towards the thorax.
- *Radioactive seed localisation (iodine-125)*: requires strict chain-of-custody documentation, dedicated staff training, radiation safety plans, and seed reconciliation at surgery and pathology. Advantages over wire include scheduling flexibility and reduced wire migration.
- *Non-radioactive markers (magnetic, radar, RFID reflectors)*: growing in use and avoid radiation governance requirements. Check MRI compatibility explicitly for each device if future breast MRI is anticipated; maintain device tracking logs.

### 11.5.6 Implants and Special Circumstances

For augmented breasts, use **Eklund (implant displacement) views** to maximise visualisation of native breast tissue; document when displacement is not achievable. Modify compression carefully — implant rupture from mammographic compression is rare but a recognised complication. Tissue expanders with magnetic ports may be MRI-unsafe: verify device details with the manufacturer before booking MRI.

In lactating patients, increased vascularity and ductal dilatation increase the risks of bleeding and milk fistula formation. Ultrasound is the first-line modality; if biopsy is needed, counsel patients accordingly.

### 11.5.7 Complications and Aftercare

- **Haematoma**: the commonest complication. Apply prolonged compression immediately post-procedure. Give clear written advice on expected bruising, signs of expanding haematoma, and when and how to seek help.
- **Infection**: uncommon. Provide a clear contact route for patients who develop signs of infection in the days following biopsy.
- **Vasovagal episodes**: recognise the prodrome (pallor, diaphoresis, nausea, bradycardia) and act before loss of consciousness — position supine, elevate legs, monitor. Most resolve without intervention.
- **Clip information**: give patients written information confirming that their clip is MRI compatible (virtually all modern biopsy site markers are) and that it may trigger airport security detectors, so they should carry documentation.

## 11.6 Pathway Safety: Interpretation, Concordance and Failsafe

### 11.6.1 Triple Assessment and the 5-Point Scoring System

The UK P/M/U/B scoring system provides a structured language for concordance:

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 1 | Normal |
| 2 | Benign |
| 3 | Uncertain/probably benign |
| 4 | Suspicious of malignancy |
| 5 | Malignant |

For B codes: B1 = normal/inadequate; B2 = benign; B3 = uncertain malignant potential; B4 = suspicious; B5 = malignant (B5a in situ, B5b invasive).

Concordance means that scores across the three components tell a coherent clinical story. Discordance demands action. A practical example: an M4/U4 spiculated mass yielding B2 (benign) on core biopsy is discordant — the histology does not explain the imaging findings, and the lesion should be re-sampled (ideally by VAB) or excised. Never accept a benign biopsy result without asking whether it explains the imaging abnormality.

### 11.6.2 Screening Interpretation

Double reading with arbitration is the structural backbone of screening quality. Individual readers and units should monitor:

- **Recall rate** (target varies; above 7% in prevalent round raises concern)
- **Cancer detection rate** and invasive:DCIS ratio
- **Positive predictive value** of recall and biopsy (PPV1, PPV2)
- **Interval cancer rate** — the most direct measure of screening miss rate
- **Small cancer rates** (<15 mm invasive) as a surrogate for stage at detection

PERFORMS provides objective external proficiency data; engage with the feedback actively. Systematic differences between readers are identified through PERFORMS and unit-level double-read discrepancy analysis.

### 11.6.3 Symptomatic Services

Safety-net appointments and robust tracking of patients who do not attend are essential — delayed diagnosis in symptomatic disease is frequently associated with failures in tracking rather than diagnostic error per se. Reports should use structured language: M and U scores with a description of the dominant finding (morphology, distribution, associated features), an explicit management recommendation, and clear communication of urgency. Vague reports that leave the MDT to infer a management plan are a quality and safety failure.

### 11.6.4 Radiology–Pathology Concordance

Monitor and audit **upgrade rates** for B3 and B4 lesions. For B3 categories — radial scars, atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), flat epithelial atypia (FEA), lobular neoplasia, papillary lesions — upgrade rates at excision vary substantially by lesion subtype and sampling method, and UK practice regarding excision versus surveillance is evolving. Decisions should be protocol-driven and made at MDT rather than unilaterally. For calcifications, VAB consistently provides superior diagnostic yield compared with standard core biopsy and is the preferred technique for calcification assessment.

## 11.7 Human Factors, Communication and Patient Experience

Most diagnostic errors and procedural incidents in radiology have a human factors component: distraction, time pressure, confirmation bias, poor handover, or ambiguous labelling. Systems should be designed to compensate for predictable human limitations:

- Standardise laterality checks and labelling at every step
- Use pre-injection and pre-biopsy time-outs as a routine habit, not an exception
- Provide written information and clear contact routes — "no news is good news" is not a safe results policy
- **Duty of Candour**: when harm occurs — a missed cancer, an SAUE, a procedural complication — the duty is to say sorry, explain what is known, outline next steps, and document. The focus should be on learning, not blame.
- Offer and document chaperones; ensure capacity assessment and use interpreters for patients with language needs
- Adapt positioning, communication, and pathway design for disability, frailty, neurodiversity, and cultural preferences
- For transgender and non-binary patients, base screening and diagnostic pathways on **anatomy and individual risk** rather than gender marker alone — this requires active attention in both IT systems and clinical practice

## 11.8 Equipment Lifecycle, IT and Data Safety

Acceptance testing and commissioning before clinical go-live establishes a documented baseline and ensures equipment meets required standards before patients are exposed. After software updates, hardware changes, or major service, re-testing is mandatory — not optional.

PACS, RIS, and NHSBSP/NBSS systems must have robust patient identity management, standardised hanging protocols, and secure mechanisms for transferring external images. Version control for reports prevents ambiguity about which report version was clinically acted upon.

Display devices require periodic physics testing and daily user checks; failing monitors should be replaced promptly rather than reported and tolerated.

Data governance under UK GDPR and the Caldicott Principles requires role-based access, secure storage of biopsy images and specimen radiographs, and clear retention and deletion policies. Sharing images across sites (for example for MDT or second opinion) should use IHE-conformant or equivalent secure methods.

## 11.9 Mobile Screening and Workplace Safety

Mobile mammography units introduce specific hazards: vehicle stability, generator safety, radiation leakage, IT connectivity, and lone-working policies all require explicit assessment and documentation. Access control to the unit during exposures must be enforced as rigorously as in a fixed facility.

Ergonomics deserves emphasis: radiographers performing mammography and sonographers performing breast ultrasound are at substantially elevated risk of work-related musculoskeletal injury. Adjustable equipment, task rotation, micro-breaks, and formal ergonomics training are not luxuries. As a radiologist, you should be aware of this when reviewing service design and understand that sonographer wellbeing directly affects service sustainability and scan quality.

## 11.10 Audit and Continuous Improvement

Audit closes the loop between performance and improvement. The metrics that matter most at unit level:

**Screening services**
- Recall rate, cancer detection rate, interval cancer rate, PPV1/PPV2
- Benign biopsy rate, small invasive cancer rates (<15 mm)
- Dose metrics (AGD distribution, outliers)
- PGMI distribution and reject/repeat analysis

**Symptomatic services**
- Time from referral to first appointment, biopsy, and result
- Re-attendance rate for repeat imaging
- B3/B4 upgrade rates, concordance rates
- Benign open biopsy rate and VAB utilisation
- Complication rates

Regular peer review of discrepant cases — across modalities and between readers — should be structured and produce documented action logs. Thematic analysis of near-misses and complaints should feed directly into training and service redesign.

**Introducing new technology**

When adopting DBT, CEM, AI-assisted reading, or novel biopsy devices, apply formal change control: risk assessment, staff training, pilot audit with prospective data collection, and staged roll-out. Monitor the actual impact on recall rates, reading times, dose, and cancer detection — do not assume published trial data directly translates to your local population and workflow.

## 11.11 Pitfalls, Misses and How to Avoid Them

These are the most instructive cases in any breast radiology service. The following are recurring patterns:

**Positioning**
- *Pitfall*: missing posterior or inferior tissue on CC or MLO — the zones where interval cancers are disproportionately found
- *Avoidance*: use contralateral comparison, targeted coaching, and repeat the view at the time if criteria are not met

**Microcalcifications**
- *Pitfall*: motion blur or inappropriate magnification technique obscuring morphology, leading to under-scoring
- *Avoidance*: stabilise the breast effectively, use short exposure times, correct magnification geometry, and consider DBT or CEM for problem-solving ambiguous calcifications

**Architectural distortion**
- *Pitfall*: skin folds and converging Cooper's ligaments mimic radial architectural distortion
- *Avoidance*: confirm taut skin and straightened folds at the time of acquisition; use spot compression views and DBT to distinguish true distortion from summation

**Ultrasound**
- *Pitfall*: compound imaging or excessive gain settings mask peripheral spiculation; failure to scan radially misses small intraductal lesions
- *Avoidance*: optimise presets for the specific clinical question; always scan radially with respect to the nipple; correlate explicitly with mammographic clockface and distance

**MRI**
- *Pitfall*: background parenchymal enhancement mistaken for non-mass enhancement (NME); subtraction artefacts simulating true enhancement
- *Avoidance*: review native T1 and T2 series alongside subtractions; schedule premenopausal women in the second week of the menstrual cycle when BPE is typically lowest; assess kinetics on subtracted source images

**Procedures**
- *Pitfall*: targeting the wrong calcification cluster; clip misplacement; under-sampling of radial scars or atypical intraductal epithelial proliferations (AIDEP) with standard core biopsy
- *Avoidance*: meticulous lesion-to-specimen correlation; use VAB for all radial scars and for discordant lesions; perform post-biopsy imaging to document clip position before the patient leaves

## 11.12 Summary

Quality assurance and safety are woven into every step of UK breast imaging — from equipment commissioning and daily constancy checks, through anatomy-driven positioning and exposure optimisation, to double reading, triple assessment concordance, and MDT decision-making. They are as much about people and processes as they are about physics.

**Key principles to carry forward:**

- **Optimise image quality** through anatomy-informed positioning, appropriate compression, and well-maintained, regularly tested equipment. Understand why a substandard image is substandard.
- **Justify and optimise every exposure.** Know your dose metrics, DRLs, and IR(ME)R obligations. Know what constitutes an SAUE and how to manage one.
- **Make procedures safe** through systematic identification checks, anticoagulation planning, aseptic technique, real-time needle visualisation, and clear aftercare communication.
- **Use P/M/U/B scoring to anchor concordance.** When scores are discordant, escalate. Never accept a histology result that does not explain the imaging findings.
- **Audit continuously.** Where practice varies — and it does — make the reasoning explicit, measure the impact, and adjust.

By understanding the *why* as well as the *how*, you will be equipped to apply safe, high-quality practice to new technologies, unfamiliar cases, and complex clinical scenarios — sustaining the trust that underpins the NHS breast screening and symptomatic pathway.
