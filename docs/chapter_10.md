# Chapter 10: Screening and Symptomatic Interpretation

---

## Overview

Breast imaging in the UK operates across two distinct but interconnected pathways: the **NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP)**, which detects early, asymptomatic cancer in average-risk women through population screening; and the **symptomatic service**, which evaluates patients with breast complaints or high-risk backgrounds through triple assessment. These pathways share modalities and scoring systems but differ fundamentally in prior probability, recall thresholds, and the weight given to clinical context.

As a core trainee, your goal is not to memorise a catalogue of appearances but to understand *why* findings look the way they do — how breast anatomy, tissue composition, and modality physics interact to produce the images you see — and to translate that understanding into transparent, reproducible judgements about the likelihood of malignancy. This chapter teaches that reasoning, anchored throughout in UK practice.

## The UK Pathways at a Glance

### NHS Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP)

The NHSBSP invites women aged 50 to their 71st birthday every three years. Many areas operate age extensions (typically 47–73 years). Eligibility for trans and non-binary people depends on sex registered at birth, gender-affirming treatment, and local policy — always check your programme's guidance.

The standard modality is **two-view digital mammography** (craniocaudal [CC] and mediolateral oblique [MLO]). Women with implants should have Eklund (implant-displacement) views wherever possible to maximise visible tissue. Every screen is read independently by **two trained readers**, with a third reader arbitrating any discordant outcome. This double-reading model exists because single-reader sensitivity for breast cancer is imperfect; the combination improves both detection and specificity.

Programme performance is measured by **recall rate**, **cancer detection rate (CDR)**, **positive predictive value (PPV) of recall**, and **interval cancer rate**. These metrics are in deliberate tension: driving recall rate down improves specificity but risks missing cancers; pushing it up increases sensitivity but generates unnecessary anxiety and procedures. Your recall threshold in screening should therefore be set conservatively — do not recall for findings that are straightforwardly benign, but do not let a bias towards low recall override genuine concern.

Recalled women attend an **assessment clinic** offering tailored additional imaging (further mammographic views, tomosynthesis, targeted ultrasound) and, where indicated, image-guided tissue sampling, all within a triple-assessment framework.

### Symptomatic Service and Triple Assessment

Symptomatic patients are referred via urgent two-week-wait or routine pathways to a one-stop clinic. The pre-test probability of malignancy is higher here than in a screening cohort, which justifies a more liberal biopsy threshold. The clinical and imaging workup is driven by the presenting complaint rather than by a fixed protocol.

**Triple assessment** combines three independent pillars:

- **Clinical examination**, scored P1–P5.
- **Imaging** — mammography (M1–M5) and/or ultrasound (U1–U5), with MRI selectively.
- **Histopathology** (B1–B5), obtained where the combined assessment warrants it.

The five-point scale runs:

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 1 | Normal |
| 2 | Benign |
| 3 | Indeterminate / probably benign |
| 4 | Suspicious of malignancy |
| 5 | Highly suspicious / malignant |

For histology, **B2** is benign, **B3** is a lesion of uncertain malignant potential, **B4** is suspicious, **B5a** is DCIS, and **B5b** is invasive carcinoma.

The key principle is that **all three pillars are assessed independently and then reconciled**. Discordance — for example, a clinically suspicious P4 lump with a seemingly benign M2/U2 image — is a red flag, not a reassurance, and must be actively resolved. Safe discharge requires concordant benign findings across all pillars.

## Anatomy and Physics: Why Things Look the Way They Do

### Breast Anatomy Relevant to Interpretation

The breast is a radially organised glandular organ, with lobules and ducts converging towards the nipple–areolar complex (NAC) and embedded in fat. **Cooper's ligaments** run from the dermis to the pectoral fascia, maintaining architecture. When a desmoplastic tumour infiltrates and contracts these ligaments, it produces the spiculated, stellate appearance characteristic of invasive carcinoma — the spicules are not tumour itself but distorted ligaments and fibrous stroma. This is why spiculation is such a powerful sign even when the central mass is tiny.

Breast density reflects the ratio of fibroglandular to fatty tissue. Dense breasts attenuate more X-rays, reducing the contrast between tumour and background. This is not merely a statistical curiosity: a mass of the same size and density is genuinely harder to see against a dense background, which is why dense breast tissue is both a risk factor (through associated biological changes) and a limitation of mammography.

### How Modality Physics Generates Appearances

**Mammography** is a projection technique — it sums attenuation along the entire X-ray path. This makes it excellent for detecting microcalcifications and for spotting the subtle retraction of architectural distortion. Its key weakness is superimposition: normal overlapping structures can mimic a mass, and a real mass can be hidden behind dense tissue. Never forget that a negative mammogram does not exclude a cancer, particularly in a dense breast.

**Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT)** acquires images at multiple angles and reconstructs thin slices, partially removing the superimposition problem. It is superior to standard 2D mammography for characterising masses and distortion. However, it is *less* sensitive than magnified 2D for fine microcalcifications because the dose constraints used in reconstruction affect fine-detail rendering. The clinical implication: use magnified 2D views whenever calcifications are your primary concern; DBT is not a substitute.

**Ultrasound** reflects sound at acoustic impedance interfaces. Malignant masses tend to be irregular, taller than they are wide (non-parallel), and produce posterior acoustic shadowing through desmoplastic attenuation. Benign lesions are typically oval, parallel to the skin, circumscribed, and homogeneously hypoechoic. These are tendencies, not rules, and the strength of ultrasound lies in how well the overall constellation of features fits a benign or malignant pattern. Ultrasound cannot reliably detect microcalcifications (the calcium clusters are too small) and is operator-dependent.

**MRI** uses dynamic gadolinium enhancement and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) to exploit the increased vascularity and altered extracellular space of malignant tissue. It has the highest sensitivity of any breast imaging modality but its specificity is context-dependent. It is not used in routine screening for average-risk women but is central to high-risk surveillance and specific problem-solving scenarios.

**Contrast-enhanced mammography (CEM)** uses dual-energy technique with iodinated contrast to generate an enhancement map overlaid on the structural mammogram. It is a pragmatic alternative to MRI in some problem-solving and staging contexts and is available in an increasing number of UK centres.

## Principles of Screening Interpretation

### Prior Probability and the Threshold Question

The screening population is asymptomatic and at average risk. Cancer prevalence is low. The purpose of recall is to bring forward to assessment only those women in whom the balance of probability and consequence justifies further investigation. This means your **recall threshold should be specific**: avoid recalling women for findings that are demonstrably benign (classic vascular calcifications, stable calcified fibroadenoma, intramammary lymph node), but do recall findings that cannot be confidently attributed to a benign process. The anxiety and inconvenience of recall are real harms, even though most recalled women are reassured; unnecessary recall also consumes assessment clinic capacity. However, the asymmetry of consequences means that, on balance, you should be more willing to recall than to miss.

### Systematic Search Pattern

Read every mammogram the same way every time. A consistent search pattern is your protection against the natural tendency to fixate on obvious findings and miss subtle ones. A reasonable sequence:

1. Check **image quality and positioning** — adequate posterior tissue, nipple in profile, pectoralis to nipple level on MLO, no motion blur.
2. Assess **density** and note if this limits sensitivity.
3. Scan for **global symmetry** on both views.
4. Focus on the **subareolar and NAC region**.
5. Work through **each quadrant systematically**, including the axillary tail.
6. Examine the **skin, trabecular pattern, and axillae**.
7. **Compare with prior images** — change over time is one of the most powerful discriminators you have. A finding that has been stable for years is almost always benign; a new or developing finding demands explanation.

### Key Screening Findings

#### Masses

Describe masses by shape (round/oval/irregular), margin (circumscribed, obscured, indistinct, microlobulated, or spiculated), and density relative to fibroglandular tissue. The margin is the single most important mammographic descriptor for predicting malignancy.

A **circumscribed, round or oval, equal- or low-density mass** that is stable over time is almost always benign — fibroadenoma, cyst, intramammary lymph node, hamartoma. **Spiculation**, however, commands attention regardless of mass size; even a 5 mm spiculated lesion has a high likelihood of malignancy because the spiculation reflects desmoplasia, not artefact. **High density** relative to surrounding fibroglandular tissue is a worrying feature, as most cancers are denser than the background parenchyma.

Ultrasound is invaluable in assessment: it immediately distinguishes a simple cyst (trivially benign and needs no further action) from a solid mass, and it refines the margin assessment. DBT can clarify whether an obscured or indistinct margin on 2D is genuinely suspicious or simply the result of overlapping tissue.

#### Calcifications

This is the area where mammography has no rival — ultrasound and MRI are largely insensitive to microcalcifications. Your analysis rests on two axes: **morphology** and **distribution**.

Morphologically, calcifications exist on a spectrum. At the benign end: skin calcifications, vascular tramlines, coarse 'popcorn' calcifications of involuting fibroadenomas, eggshell or rim calcifications of fat necrosis, and milk-of-calcium (which layers on the lateral view, creating the classic 'teacup' appearance, and appears smudgy on the CC view). At the suspicious end: **fine pleomorphic** or **fine linear/branching (casting)** calcifications, which form within distorted or necrotic ductal epithelium in high-grade DCIS.

Distribution refines the picture. Scattered bilateral calcifications are almost invariably benign. A **segmental or linear** distribution, tracking along a ductal tree, markedly raises concern for DCIS. Clustered calcifications in a small volume are indeterminate when morphology is heterogeneous fine granular, and suspicious when morphology is pleomorphic or linear/branching.

Practical points: always obtain **magnification views** in at least two projections before making a decision about calcifications; a lateral view distinguishes milk-of-calcium layering; new or increasing calcifications are more concerning than established, unchanged ones.

#### Architectural Distortion

Architectural distortion is a focal disruption of the normal parenchymal pattern — lines radiating from a point, or focal retraction — without a visible central mass. It is one of the commonest mammographic findings to be missed, particularly in the upper inner quadrant and against a dense background.

The principal cause of concern is malignancy, particularly **invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC)**, which infiltrates without forming a discrete mass. Other causes include radial scar/complex sclerosing lesion (CSL), postsurgical scar, and fat necrosis. New distortion in a woman without prior surgery is suspicious until proved otherwise. DBT substantially improves both detection and characterisation of distortion by removing overlapping tissue. On ultrasound, correlating distortion can be subtle — look for an ill-defined hypoechoic area with posterior shadowing rather than a discrete mass.

#### Asymmetries

A **global asymmetry** (one breast larger than the other) is usually a normal variant. A **focal asymmetry** (a discrete area of increased density compared to the mirror-image site) warrants careful evaluation, particularly if new or developing. A **developing asymmetry** — one that is new or increasing compared to priors — is the highest-risk subtype and should almost always prompt assessment.

Many asymmetries resolve on spot compression views or DBT, confirming summation artefact. Those that persist and are associated with distortion, palpable change, or indeterminate morphology require ultrasound and often biopsy.

#### Associated Signs

Always survey the skin, trabecular pattern, nipple, and axillae. Skin thickening and trabecular oedema suggest lymphatic obstruction, which may be malignant or inflammatory. Unilateral findings are more concerning than bilateral. Nipple inversion of new onset is suspicious. An axillary node that has lost its hilar lucency and become rounded or enlarged deserves ultrasound assessment. These secondary signs are sometimes the first clue to a cancer not visible elsewhere on the image.

## Principles of Symptomatic Interpretation

### Different Context, Different Mindset

In the symptomatic clinic, the presenting complaint determines the focus of imaging, and the prior probability of malignancy is meaningfully higher than in a screening cohort. Your imaging strategy is guided by age, symptom, and risk: **ultrasound is generally first-line under age 35**; **mammography is added from around 40 onwards**, sometimes from 35 depending on clinical concern; MRI is used selectively. The symptomatic area must be targeted, but never neglect the rest of the breast — the 'second lesion' is a real phenomenon.

### Assigning UK Scores Explicitly

In every symptomatic assessment, assign **P, M, and U scores independently**, document them in your report, and state whether they are concordant. This is not a bureaucratic exercise: it forces you to consider each piece of evidence separately before combining it, and it creates a transparent paper trail for the MDT. An M3 or U3 score is not an admission of ignorance; it is a probabilistic statement that warrants proportionate action, usually biopsy.

### Common Symptomatic Presentations

#### Palpable Lump

This is the most common referral. Under 35, begin with ultrasound; mammography is added only if ultrasound is suspicious or indeterminate, or if there are strong risk factors. Over 40, perform two-view mammography and targeted ultrasound routinely. Between 35 and 39, use clinical judgement.

The critical rule: **never dismiss a palpable lump on the basis of apparently benign imaging alone**. A clinically suspicious P4 lump with a U2/M2 image is discordant — the discordance must be resolved, not explained away. Conversely, if imaging reveals a suspicious lesion in an area the patient cannot feel, biopsy the imaging target regardless.

#### Breast Pain

Diffuse cyclical mastalgia without focal findings in a young woman rarely requires imaging and can be managed with reassurance. Focal, persistent, non-cyclical pain warrants targeted ultrasound (add mammography aged 40 and over). Always consider extramammary causes — costochondritis and musculoskeletal pain are common. Inflammatory carcinoma can present with pain alongside erythema, oedema, and peau d'orange; if this combination is present, treat it as an oncological emergency.

#### Nipple Discharge

**Physiological** (bilateral, milky or green, expressible) discharge rarely needs imaging unless atypical for the patient. **Pathological** discharge is spontaneous, unilateral, from a single duct, and bloody or serous — this pattern needs imaging (ultrasound of the subareolar region; mammography aged 40 and over) and, if imaging is negative, consideration of duct excision. Intraductal papilloma is the commonest cause and typically yields a B3 histology result; management (vacuum-assisted excision versus surgical excision) depends on the presence of atypia and local MDT preference.

#### Infection and Inflammation

Ultrasound confirms an abscess and guides aspiration or drain placement. In **non-lactational mastitis**, particularly in smokers, always consider periductal mastitis and its complications, and ensure the inflammation resolves completely before discharging — underlying malignancy must be excluded in atypical or non-resolving cases.

**Inflammatory breast carcinoma** is a clinical and radiological emergency: rapid onset of erythema, oedema, peau d'orange, and skin thickening. Mammography and ultrasound typically show skin thickening, trabecular oedema, and often an underlying mass; biopsy should include both breast tissue and skin.

#### Nipple and Areolar Changes

Paget's disease of the nipple presents as unilateral eczematous change of the nipple itself (not the areola), which does not respond to topical treatment. Biopsy the nipple lesion and perform mammography and ultrasound to search for underlying DCIS or invasive disease. New nipple inversion prompts full two-view mammography and targeted ultrasound.

#### Pregnancy and Lactation

Ultrasound is first-line. Mammography is not absolutely contraindicated — fetal dose is negligible — but use it only when the clinical picture justifies it, as density is high and sensitivity reduced. Common incidental findings include galactoceles (fat–fluid level on ultrasound and mammography) and lactational adenomas. Importantly, cancer does occur during pregnancy and lactation; do not attribute every palpable mass to pregnancy-related change without adequate assessment.

## Mammographic Appearances in Detail

### Masses

The single most useful skill is understanding the continuum from circumscribed to spiculated. A **circumscribed margin** — where you can draw a clean line around the lesion — is associated with pushing-type growth (fibroadenoma, cyst, metastasis, lymphoma). A **spiculated margin** reflects infiltrative growth with desmoplasia and is highly predictive of malignancy even in a small lesion. Between these extremes, **indistinct** and **microlobulated** margins are intermediate in risk; **obscured** margins may reflect benign superimposition or genuine ill-definition, which DBT can distinguish.

Specific diagnostic challenges:

- **Fibroadenoma versus phyllodes tumour**: both typically circumscribed. Rapid growth, large size (>3 cm is a loose threshold), lobulated contour, or heterogeneous internal architecture on ultrasound should raise concern about phyllodes; histology is required.
- **Fat necrosis**: produces a spectrum including oil cysts (lucent with thin rim), rim calcifications, and — importantly — distortion with spiculation in the early or involuting phase that can convincingly mimic invasive carcinoma. A history of trauma, surgery, or radiotherapy is helpful, but when imaging is discordant or the history absent, biopsy is appropriate.

### Calcifications

Apply the descriptors consistently: morphology first, then distribution. Know the classic benign patterns well enough that you can confidently leave them unreported (vascular tramlines, skin calcifications in profile, teacup milk-of-calcium). When you see **fine pleomorphic** or **fine linear/branching** calcifications in a **segmental or linear** distribution, recall is warranted; the likelihood of DCIS is high.

Always magnify. A cluster of granular calcifications that looks indeterminate at standard magnification may reveal clearly pleomorphic morphology or branching forms on a well-performed magnification view. Use the lateral view to check for layering, which identifies milk-of-calcium and avoids unnecessary recall.

### Architectural Distortion

Systematically look for the combination of focal convergence and retraction. The challenge is distinguishing true distortion from the normal wavy interdigitation of fibroglandular tissue, which is easier with DBT. Postsurgical scars tend to have a central lucent zone, are located at the expected site of prior surgery, and are stable or regressing over time. New distortion remote from any surgical site, or progressively increasing distortion, demands investigation.

## Ultrasound Correlation

### Solid Masses

The **ACR lexicon** terms apply in UK practice. Benign features: oval shape, circumscribed margin, parallel orientation (wider than tall), homogeneous echotexture, thin echogenic pseudocapsule. Suspicious features: irregular or spiculated margins, non-parallel orientation (taller than wide), heterogeneous echotexture, posterior shadowing, echogenic halo, chaotic vascularity on Doppler. None of these features is pathognomonic in isolation; the gestalt of the whole constellation guides the U score.

Simple cysts need no intervention. Complicated cysts (homogeneous low-level echoes, no mural nodules) are almost always benign but may warrant short-term follow-up. Complex cystic lesions with thick walls, thick septations, or vascular mural nodules are concerning and require biopsy.

### Axillary Assessment

A normal lymph node is oval, has a cortex of less than 3 mm, preserves its fatty hilum, and shows hilar-pattern vascularity. Cortical thickening, eccentric bulge, a round shape, loss of hilum, or non-hilar blood flow are suspicious features that should prompt fine-needle aspiration cytology or core biopsy. Node sampling is important for staging and for planning surgery (sentinel node biopsy versus axillary clearance); clip marking aids subsequent localisation.

In UK practice, **breast MRI** is used for:

- Occult primary cancer presenting with axillary nodal metastases.
- Extent of disease assessment, particularly in ILC where mammography consistently underestimates spread.
- Problem-solving when conventional imaging and histology are discordant.
- Assessment of response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
- High-risk screening (a separate, dedicated pathway).

MRI interpretation combines **morphological assessment** (mass versus non-mass enhancement, margin, internal architecture) with **kinetic analysis** (rapid initial enhancement with washout is more suspicious; persistent enhancement is more likely benign). DWI adds value: restricted diffusion at high b-values supports malignancy. Crucially, no single MRI feature is specific enough to diagnose or exclude cancer; the whole picture, correlated with conventional imaging and biopsy, determines management.

**CEM** is a practical alternative to MRI in many problem-solving scenarios and is increasingly used in extent assessment and some staging contexts. Interpret the low-energy (structural) images alongside the subtracted enhancement map: the combination provides both morphology and physiological information in one examination.

## Image-Guided Biopsy and Pathology Correlation

### Choosing the Right Guidance Modality

When a sonographic correlate exists, **ultrasound-guided core biopsy** is the default: it is real-time, accurate, comfortable for the patient, and avoids radiation. When a lesion is visible only on mammography — calcifications, distortion, or non-calcified non-mass lesions — **stereotactic or DBT-guided vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB)** is preferred. VAB's larger sample volume and ability to target and confirm calcification retrieval makes it superior to standard core biopsy for this indication. **MRI-guided biopsy** is reserved for lesions visible only on MRI in centres with the capability and where the result would change management.

### The B Category and Its Consequences

- **B2**: concordant benign findings permit safe discharge.
- **B3** (uncertain malignant potential): this category encompasses a heterogeneous group — atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), classical LCIS, radial scar/CSL, papilloma, flat epithelial atypia (FEA), and mucocele-like lesions. The key issue is **upgrade risk** to in-situ or invasive carcinoma on excision, which varies by lesion type and sampling method. Management is nuanced and evolving; MDT discussion is mandatory. Many B3 lesions are managed by vacuum-assisted excision rather than open surgery, but atypia or discordance may warrant surgical excision.
- **B4**: suspicious — proceed to definitive excision or targeted repeat sampling.
- **B5a/B5b**: treat per MDT.

### Concordance: The Central Safety Principle

After every biopsy, ask yourself: *does this pathology result explain what I see on imaging?* A B2 result for a spiculated mass is **non-concordant**; the correct response is repeat biopsy or excision, not discharge. Concordance is the safety net of the triple-assessment system, and recognising non-concordance is one of the most important skills you will develop as a breast radiologist.

## Technical and Interpretive Pitfalls

**Positioning errors** are common and consequential: inadequate posterior tissue on CC, insufficient pectoralis on MLO, nipple not in profile, and absent inframammary fold all hide tissue. Never accept a poor-quality image if repeat acquisition is safe and feasible.

**Summation artefact** is the single most common cause of false-positive recall. Overlapping normal structures can convincingly mimic a mass on one view but disappear on an orthogonal view, spot-compression view, or DBT slice. Develop the habit of checking whether a 'finding' on one view has a correlate on the other before raising concern.

**Skin lesions** — seborrhoeic keratoses, moles, neurofibromas — project as ill-defined calcified or dense foci. A skin marker or tangential view immediately resolves the question.

**Common misses** include: subtle architectural distortion (especially in dense, upper-inner-quadrant tissue), small clusters of fine calcifications on suboptimal images, medial breast cancers on the CC view when medial tissue is inadequate, and interval cancers that in retrospect show the earliest signs of a rapidly developing high-grade tumour. Reviewing interval cancers — cases where the woman presented symptomatically between screens — is one of the best educational tools for learning to recognise nascent abnormalities.

**Post-treatment change** — scar, fat necrosis, radiation oedema — can mimic malignancy. Key features favouring post-treatment benign change are: central fat density, rim calcification, stability or regression over time, and a predictable anatomical location. When in doubt, biopsy.

## Multi-Modality Correlation

Understanding how different tumour subtypes behave across modalities strengthens your interpretation of each individually:

**Mass-forming invasive ductal carcinoma (no special type)**: spiculated, high-density mass on mammography; irregular, non-parallel, shadowing mass on ultrasound; focal convergence on DBT; desmoplastic stroma on histology. The imaging and pathology tell the same biological story.

**DCIS**: typically presents as pleomorphic or fine linear/branching calcifications in segmental distribution on mammography. Often invisible on ultrasound unless associated with a palpable mass or significant ductal dilatation. On MRI or CEM, clumped or linear/segmental non-mass enhancement is characteristic. High-grade DCIS with comedo necrosis is most likely to produce casting-type calcifications.

**Invasive lobular carcinoma**: the great masquerader. Infiltrates as single files of cells with minimal desmoplasia, producing subtle asymmetry or distortion rather than a discrete mass. Mammography consistently underestimates extent. Ultrasound may show only an ill-defined hypoechoic area with shadowing, or be entirely normal. MRI is the most sensitive modality for extent assessment and should be strongly considered before surgery.

## Reporting, Communication, and MDT

Every report — screening or symptomatic — should include an explicit P/M/U score, the location and size of any finding, clear descriptors, and an unambiguous recommendation. At post-biopsy MDT, document the concordance statement explicitly.

In screening, be mindful of the **anxiety generated by recall**. Recall letters and clinic communications should be clear and avoid language that catastrophises. In symptomatic clinics, obtaining consent for biopsy includes explaining clip placement and the possibility of further procedures — this is part of good clinical practice, not an optional extra.

The **MDT is not a rubber stamp**: it is the mechanism for reconciling imaging, pathology, and surgical/oncological expertise, and for resolving discordance. Bring difficult cases, non-concordant results, and cases where you are uncertain; that is precisely what the MDT is for.

## Quality Assurance and Governance

Good breast radiology is inseparable from good governance. In screening, track your own **recall rate, CDR, interval cancer rate**, and double-reading discordance. In symptomatic practice, audit the **PPV of biopsy, benign-to-malignant biopsy ratio, B3 upgrade rate, and concordance rate**. These metrics are not performance-management tools — they are feedback that calibrates your threshold and technique.

**Significant event analysis** of unexpected outcomes (missed cancers, complications, non-concordant results) should be approached as a learning opportunity rather than a blame exercise. Structured case review and regular consensus meetings keep thresholds aligned across the team and accelerate learning in a way that solitary practice cannot.

## Putting It All Together: Practical Judgement

A few principles to carry forward:

- **Screening and symptomatic practice require different mindsets.** Adjust your threshold consciously when you move between them.
- **Understand the biology before the appearances.** Spiculation means desmoplasia; segmental calcification means ductal DCIS; subtle asymmetry may mean lobular infiltration. When you know why things look as they do, you can generalise to unusual cases.
- **Be explicit about uncertainty.** An M3 or U3 score communicates a probabilistic judgement that leads to proportionate action. It is not a failure of diagnosis — it is honest, and it protects patients.
- **Use change over time.** A new or growing finding is almost always more important than an established stable one.
- **Concordance is your safety net.** When pathology does not explain imaging, escalate: repeat biopsy, MDT discussion, or excision.
- **Know when to stop.** Unequivocally benign findings do not need over-investigation. A stable, calcified fibroadenoma in a screened woman is a normal variant, not a biopsy target.

Breast radiology rewards intellectual rigour, systematic practice, and a willingness to sit with uncertainty — and then act on it appropriately. These habits, built now as a core trainee, will serve you throughout your career.
