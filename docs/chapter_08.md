# Chapter 8: Structured Reporting

## Why structured reporting matters

Structured reporting is not an administrative exercise — it is a discipline that forces you to answer the clinical question, describe findings reproducibly, categorise risk explicitly, and specify the next action. Done well, it reduces errors of omission, enables meaningful double reading and arbitration, supports imaging–pathology concordance review, and provides the data substrate for NHSBSP audit (recall positive predictive values, interval cancer rates). For trainees, it also encodes a systematic approach that transfers across modalities and case types.

In UK breast practice, every report must dovetail with the triple assessment framework: clinical examination scored as **P1–P5**, mammography as **M1–M5**, ultrasound as **U1–U5**, and histology as **B1–B5**. The purpose is not merely description but coherent decision-making — a single, unambiguous overall category and a clear management plan that can be enacted at the multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting or immediately at the assessment clinic.

## Where structured reporting fits in the UK pathway

Understanding the context before you report is essential, because the structure and emphasis differ between settings.

**Screening (NHSBSP):** Women aged 50–70 are invited three-yearly for two-view mammography. Reports must be concise, codeable into the National Breast Screening System (NBSS), and communicate a binary recall/no-recall decision with a specific reason for recall. Assessment clinic reports then describe targeted imaging and biopsy findings with an outcome and discharge or referral decision.

**Symptomatic (triple assessment):** The report integrates with a same-day workflow. It must provide M and U scores that complement the clinical P-score, state whether biopsy is indicated, and — once histology returns — document imaging–pathology concordance with an explicit plan.

**Additional modalities:** Tomosynthesis (DBT) is increasingly used both within screening and at assessment. MRI is used for specific indications: extent of disease in known cancer (particularly lobular carcinoma), occult primary, neoadjuvant response assessment, and implant integrity. Each modality has its own structured elements but should contribute to a single integrated impression.

## Principles of a good breast report

Before reaching for a template, internalise these principles:

- **Start with the question.** Is this a screening decision or a symptomatic problem? The answer shapes what you must include.
- **Be systematic.** Use the same order every time to reduce omissions.
- **Be selective but precise.** Report what changes management. Avoid vague hedging — it transfers uncertainty to the clinician without helping.
- **Categorise explicitly.** Assign M and U scores and mean them. Do not write M3 to avoid commitment when the evidence points to M4.
- **Correlate actively.** Compare with priors, correlate between modalities, and later with pathology. State where findings correlate and where they do not.
- **Close the loop.** A report without a category and a recommended action is incomplete.

## The UK 5-point scoring systems

These scores are not ornamental — they drive management. Understand what each category commits you to.

| Score | P (clinical) | M (mammography) | U (ultrasound) | B (histology) |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| 1 | Normal | Normal | Normal | Normal tissue / insufficient |
| 2 | Benign | Benign | Benign | Benign |
| 3 | Indeterminate | Indeterminate / probably benign | Indeterminate | Uncertain malignant potential (B3) |
| 4 | Suspicious | Suspicious | Suspicious | Suspicious of malignancy |
| 5 | Malignant | Highly suspicious | Highly suspicious | Malignant |

A few points worth understanding:

- **B1** means either normal tissue or insufficient — the distinction matters clinically. A B1 "insufficient" from a suspicious target (U4/U5) mandates repeat sampling; a B1 "normal tissue" from a targeted lesion is still discordant with high-suspicion imaging.
- **B3** (lesion of uncertain malignant potential) encompasses a heterogeneous group — lobular neoplasia, atypical ductal hyperplasia, papillary lesions, flat epithelial atypia, phyllodes tumour. Management varies by lesion type and local MDT policy; vacuum-assisted excision or diagnostic excision may be recommended. Do not lump these together in your report.
- **B5** is frequently subcategorised as **B5a** (non-invasive, e.g., DCIS) and **B5b** (invasive carcinoma). Some laboratories note microinvasion separately. Know what your local laboratory reports and reflect it accurately.
- Some centres apply an **R1–R5** scheme to MRI, or simply align MRI findings with the M/U framework. Either is acceptable provided the action is explicit. Check local practice.

The **M3/U3 category** deserves particular emphasis because it is where judgement is most tested. It should mean "genuinely indeterminate — I cannot confidently assign benign or suspicious." If you find yourself defaulting to M3 to avoid the discomfort of M4, ask yourself whether the finding warrants biopsy. If it does, call it M4 and recommend biopsy; do not underplay a suspicious finding.

## Core components of a structured breast imaging report

### 1) Administrative and clinical details

- Patient identifiers and laterality check — never skip this.
- Indication: for screening, the reason for recall (e.g., "possible calcifications left UOQ"); for symptomatic, the presenting complaint with side, clockface position, distance from nipple in centimetres, duration, and clinical P-score.
- Relevant history: age, menopausal and hormonal status, pregnancy or lactation, prior surgery or biopsy, radiotherapy, implants, high-risk status or family history. These contextualise findings — a 28-year-old pregnant woman with a hypoechoic mass needs different management from a 65-year-old with the same finding.

### 2) Prior comparison

State the dates and modalities of all relevant previous imaging, or explicitly note if none is available. Summarise the relevant change: new, increased, stable, or decreased. Where comparison is technically limited (e.g., different mammographic positioning), say so — "probably stable but technically limited comparison" is an honest and useful statement.

### 3) Technique and limitations

- **Mammography:** views obtained, whether DBT was used, magnification or spot compression views, image quality and positioning.
- **Ultrasound:** transducer frequency, whether examination was targeted or whole-breast, any adjuncts used (elastography, contrast), and any limitations (e.g., dense acoustic shadowing from a scar, poorly visualised deep tissue).
- **MRI:** field strength, protocol, timing within the menstrual cycle if the patient is premenopausal (high background parenchymal enhancement mid-cycle reduces specificity), contrast given, quality of fat suppression, and any motion artefact.
- **Interventional:** guidance modality, needle gauge, vacuum assistance, local anaesthetic used, clip type, and whether specimen radiography was performed.

### 4) Breast composition

Comment on density where it affects sensitivity or influences the interpretation. A simple statement such as "Heterogeneously dense parenchyma, which may obscure small lesions" is clinically meaningful. The NHSBSP does not currently mandate formal density notification to women, but density should be documented where relevant to the decision.

### 5) Systematic description of findings

Use the same order every time:

**Skin and nipple-areolar complex:** skin thickening or peau d'orange (diffuse vs localised), nipple retraction (new versus longstanding — ask the patient), nipple discharge features, dermal calcifications, and surgical scars.

**Parenchyma by quadrant:** masses, architectural distortion, asymmetries, calcifications, ductal changes, trabecular thickening, and oedema.

**Axilla and chest wall:** lymph node morphology (cortical thickness, hilar fat, shape, level), any extranodal extension, vessel or muscle involvement.

**Implants:** location (subglandular or subpectoral), peri-implant fluid, capsular calcification, intracapsular collapse ("linguine sign" on MRI), extracapsular silicone.

**Localisation:** for every lesion, document laterality, clockface position, distance from the nipple in centimetres, and depth (anterior/middle/posterior third, or near/mid/far). Size should be given in millimetres in three orthogonal planes where feasible. For calcifications, measure the maximum span in two planes — this drives surgical planning. For distortion and non-mass enhancement (NME), give the maximal span.

Where multiple lesions are present, number them. State explicitly whether disease is **multifocal** (multiple foci within the same quadrant) or **multicentric** (foci in separate quadrants) — this influences surgical planning.

**Modality-specific descriptors:**

*Mammography and tomosynthesis:*
- **Masses:** shape (round / oval / irregular), margins (circumscribed / obscured / microlobulated / indistinct / spiculated), density relative to fibroglandular tissue, and associated features (skin or nipple changes, trabecular thickening, oedema, calcifications).
- **Calcifications:** morphology (skin, vascular, coarse "popcorn", lucent-centred, egg-shell — these are characteristically benign; versus amorphous, coarse heterogeneous, fine pleomorphic, fine linear or branching — these raise suspicion) and distribution (diffuse, regional, grouped, linear, segmental). Segmental and linear distributions map to ductal anatomy and increase the probability of DCIS regardless of morphology.
- **Asymmetries:** document whether spot compression or DBT clarifies the finding. A "developing asymmetry" should only be labelled as such if change from prior studies is documented.

*Ultrasound:*
- **Masses:** shape, margin (circumscribed / indistinct / angular / microlobulated / spiculated), orientation (parallel versus non-parallel to skin), echogenicity (anechoic / hypoechoic / isoechoic / hyperechoic / complex), posterior features (enhancement / shadowing / no change), internal architecture, calcifications (with or without acoustic shadow), and vascularity.
- **Ducts:** dilatation, intraduct lesion, vascular stalk.
- **Nodes:** cortical thickness in millimetres, focal versus diffuse hypertrophy, hilar fat, shape, and vascularity pattern.

*MRI:*
- **Background parenchymal enhancement (BPE):** minimal / mild / moderate / marked. High BPE reduces specificity and should be noted explicitly.
- **Mass:** shape, margin, internal enhancement pattern (homogeneous / heterogeneous / rim-enhancing), kinetic curve (initial phase: slow / medium / rapid; delayed phase: persistent / plateau / washout), T2 signal, and diffusion restriction if available. Note that morphology should dominate your assessment — kinetics are supportive but are not determinative.
- **NME:** distribution (focal / linear / segmental / regional / diffuse) and internal pattern (homogeneous / heterogeneous / clumped / clustered ring). Segmental distribution with clumped or clustered ring internal pattern is the classic appearance of DCIS on MRI.
- **Focus/foci:** small enhancing points — contextualise carefully; most are benign but multiplying or new foci in a suspicious context warrant attention.

### 6) Impression and category

Provide a single, integrated conclusion. If there are multiple findings, categorise each individually, then give an overall M and/or U category that drives management. Use unambiguous language: "benign," "indeterminate — biopsy recommended," "suspicious of malignancy." Assign the category explicitly:

- **M1/U1** — normal
- **M2/U2** — benign
- **M3/U3** — indeterminate / probably benign: if you use this, justify why the finding is not clearly M2 or clearly M4
- **M4/U4** — suspicious
- **M5/U5** — highly suspicious of malignancy

### 7) Recommendation

State the next step and timescale clearly:

- Discharge to routine recall (screening) or GP (symptomatic), with safety-netting advice where appropriate
- Additional views or targeted ultrasound
- Image-guided core biopsy or vacuum-assisted biopsy (specify guidance modality and rationale)
- Clip placement
- MDT discussion
- Axillary ultrasound and needle sampling
- MRI for extent assessment or occult primary

If imaging and clinical findings are discordant, say so explicitly and propose how to resolve it — usually by escalating to biopsy or further imaging.

## Structured reporting in screening

### Double reading and arbitration

First and second reads should be concise and codeable: normal (routine recall) or recall with a specific reason. Use NBSS reason codes where available. When readers disagree, the arbitration report should record the deciding rationale — not just the outcome. Arbitration is a learning opportunity; resist the temptation to default to the more cautious reader without applying independent judgement.

### Recall assessment reports

The assessment report should:

1. Summarise the screening finding that prompted recall (side, quadrant, clockface, distance from nipple).
2. List the targeted assessment imaging performed.
3. Describe findings in structured terms with M and U categories.
4. State a clear outcome: assessment benign with discharge; indeterminate — biopsy performed; suspicious — biopsy performed; or (rarely) technical repeat required with justification.
5. Document any biopsy: guidance modality, needle gauge, clip type, clip position on post-procedure mammography, and whether specimen radiography confirmed representative calcifications.
6. State a provisional concordance expectation before histology returns — this helps the MDT anticipate discordance.

### Common pitfalls in screening reports

- **Vague recall reasons** — "possible mass" is not actionable; "12 mm oval density, right UOQ, 8 cm FN, new compared with 2021" is.
- **Failing to localise calcifications precisely** — document extent, clockface, and distance from nipple.
- **No overall category or action** after a detailed description — the description is incomplete without a conclusion.
- **Labelling a developing asymmetry** without documented change from prior rounds — if you have not seen the priors, be explicit about this uncertainty.

## Structured reporting in symptomatic triple assessment

### Integrating P, M, U, and B

Begin with the presenting symptom and the clinical P-score. A normal mammogram (M1) does not dismiss a convincing P4 or P5 clinical finding — state the discordance and escalate to targeted ultrasound and, if appropriate, biopsy. The overall assessment should integrate all available scores: the highest score generally drives management, but concordance across scores gives confidence in either direction.

Once histology returns, document the B category and an explicit concordance statement. Do not leave concordance implicit — write it out.

### Concordance in practice

Here are three paradigm cases illustrating the principle:

**Concordant benign:** *"Left 3 o'clock, 4 cm FN: U2 oval, circumscribed, homogeneous hypoechoic mass. Core biopsy B2 fibroadenoma. Concordant. Discharge with safety-netting."*

**Discordant — action required:** *"Right UOQ spiculated mass, M5. Core biopsy B2 fat necrosis. Discordant — imaging highly suspicious with benign histology. Repeat biopsy with vacuum-assisted device or proceed to diagnostic surgical excision recommended. Discuss at MDT."*

**Concordant malignant:** *"Left UOQ: M5/U5 21 mm spiculated mass. Core biopsy B5b grade 2 invasive ductal carcinoma. Concordant. Clip in situ. Refer to MDT for surgical planning."*

Discordant benign histology against M4/M5 or U4/U5 imaging is the most important concordance failure to recognise — it requires prompt escalation.

### Axillary assessment

Include a structured axillary ultrasound in any proven or suspected malignancy, or where nodes are symptomatic:

- Side, level (I–III where relevant), number of morphologically abnormal nodes.
- Cortical thickness in millimetres; focal versus diffuse hypertrophy; hilum status.
- Suspected extranodal extension.
- Whether needle sampling was performed (FNA or core), and the B category result when available.

Some centres assign a U category to nodes; others simply state "morphologically abnormal — needle sampling performed." Either is acceptable; be consistent with local practice.

## Modality-specific structured elements

### Mammography and tomosynthesis

**Masses and distortion — suspicious features:** irregular, spiculated masses; focal architectural distortion with converging lines and no evident benign cause (prior biopsy site, scar); associated skin or nipple retraction; desmoplastic reaction.

**Masses and distortion — benign features:** fat-density oil cysts with rim calcification; coarse "popcorn" calcification of an involuted fibroadenoma; focal asymmetry that effaces completely on compression and has a normal ultrasound correlate.

**Calcifications:** think about pathophysiology, not just morphology. Fine linear and branching calcifications form in necrotic intraluminal debris in high-grade DCIS — the morphology and distribution (segmental, following a duct) together explain the finding. Amorphous calcifications are less specific; context and distribution determine whether they reach M3 or M4. Always measure the span for planning purposes.

DBT can clarify architectural distortion that appears equivocal on 2D mammography — document its use and what it showed.

**Technical pitfall — mislocalising calcifications between views:** use triangulation and, if placing a clip, confirm its position on a post-procedure two-view mammogram documenting distance from the calcification in millimetres.

### Ultrasound

**Suspicious hallmarks:** irregular shape, non-parallel (taller-than-wide) orientation — this suggests growth across normal tissue planes — spiculated or angulated margins, heterogeneous echotexture, posterior acoustic shadowing, peripheral or chaotic vascularity, and an echogenic halo.

**Benign hallmarks:** oval, parallel, circumscribed, homogeneously hypoechoic mass (classic fibroadenoma); simple cyst (anechoic, imperceptibly thin wall, posterior acoustic enhancement); clustered microcysts; intramammary node with fatty hilum and thin cortex (≤3 mm).

**Indeterminate:** complicated cysts (internal echoes but otherwise cyst-like), small solid circumscribed masses in older women (where a fibroadenoma is less likely), and papillomas (intraduct solid lesion, particularly with associated nipple discharge) — explain why biopsy is preferred rather than simply assigning U3.

**Pitfalls:**
- Only measuring in two planes — always attempt three orthogonal measurements.
- Not demonstrating clip–lesion correlation after biopsy — document this explicitly on a post-procedure image.
- Reporting "no focal abnormality" when the symptomatic area was not specifically interrogated — document precisely which area was examined and acknowledge any limitations.

### MRI

MRI is a problem-solver and extent-assessor, not a screening tool (outside high-risk surveillance). Use it selectively.

**UK indications:** extent assessment in known cancer (particularly lobular histology, or where mammography and ultrasound are discordant about size), occult primary presenting with axillary nodal metastasis, response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and implant integrity assessment where ultrasound is inadequate.

**Morphology first, kinetics second.** Washout kinetics raise suspicion but they are neither sensitive nor specific enough to stand alone. A persistent kinetic curve does not make a morphologically suspicious lesion benign.

**NME and DCIS:** segmental distribution with clumped or clustered ring internal enhancement is the signature of DCIS on MRI. Recognise it and recommend appropriate biopsy — MRI-guided if no mammographic or ultrasound correlate, otherwise targeted stereotactic sampling.

**Always state whether a second-look ultrasound correlate was identified** for any MRI target. If a correlate is found on ultrasound, it can be biopsied under ultrasound guidance, which is simpler, cheaper, and more comfortable for the patient.

**Pitfall — high BPE:** when background parenchymal enhancement is marked, specificity falls and false-positive recommendations increase. Acknowledge this in the report and consider whether timing within the menstrual cycle might improve the examination.

## Image-guided biopsy: documenting what matters

A complete biopsy report includes:

- **Target:** side, clockface, distance from nipple, depth, size of lesion.
- **Guidance modality:** ultrasound, stereotactic (mammography or DBT), or MRI.
- **Technique:** needle gauge, vacuum assistance (if used), number of cores obtained, any technical challenges.
- **Clip:** type, confirmation of deployment, and distance of clip from the target on post-procedure mammography (if within the lesion or within an acceptable distance of the calcification span, state this explicitly).
- **Specimen radiography:** for calcification targets — state whether the target morphology is represented and the approximate length of calcification within the cores.
- **Complications:** haematoma, vasovagal episode, pain.
- **Provisional expectation:** state whether you anticipate benign or malignant histology, so the MDT can anticipate and identify discordance.
- **Histology addendum:** B category, diagnosis, receptor status if available, and an explicit concordance statement with the next recommended step.

**Handling discordance:**

- B2 histology against M4/M5 or U4/U5 imaging: state discordant and recommend repeat sampling with vacuum-assisted device or surgical excision.
- B3 histology: specify the lesion type, note local MDT policy (vacuum excision versus diagnostic excision), and ensure the recommended action is recorded clearly.

## Correlation, concordance, and MDT

**Cross-modality correlation** is active, not passive. State where mammographic calcifications lie relative to the ultrasound mass. If there is no ultrasound correlate for suspicious calcifications, say so explicitly — this confirms stereotactic guidance is required for biopsy.

**Imaging–pathology concordance** must be written, not assumed. If there is any uncertainty, flag it for MDT discussion.

**MDT contribution:** when presenting a known cancer, summarise imaging concisely but completely — tumour dimensions, multifocality, nipple–tumour distance, skin or chest wall involvement, nodal status, and any factors influencing conservation versus mastectomy. The surgeon and oncologist need this information to plan treatment; it should not require them to re-read the full report.

## Benign versus suspicious: structured reasoning

Understanding the **underlying pathophysiology** of imaging descriptors helps you apply them correctly in unfamiliar cases, rather than pattern-matching to memorised examples.

- **Spiculated margins** reflect a desmoplastic stromal reaction to infiltrative tumour growth — they are among the most specific mammographic signs of malignancy.
- **Non-parallel (taller-than-wide) orientation** on ultrasound suggests a lesion growing across rather than along tissue planes — a hallmark of infiltrative behaviour.
- **Fine linear and branching calcifications** represent calcified intraluminal debris in necrotic high-grade DCIS — the morphology and segmental distribution together reflect ductal anatomy.
- **Associated features** (skin thickening, nipple retraction, trabecular oedema, axillary nodal cortical thickening) rarely appear together in benign disease; their combination significantly increases the pre-test probability of malignancy.

When features conflict — a circumscribed mass on mammography that is angular on ultrasound, for example — acknowledge the discordance, explain your reasoning, and choose the action that resolves the uncertainty. In most cases that means biopsy.

## Common interpretive and technical pitfalls

- **Laterality and location errors:** always cross-check identifiers before dictating; use clockface and distance from nipple for every lesion.
- **Missing architectural distortion:** develop a systematic search pattern; use DBT to resolve summation artefact when equivocal on 2D.
- **Under-calling new asymmetries:** if prior studies are limited, be honest — "no reliable comparison available; developing asymmetry cannot be excluded" is more useful than false reassurance.
- **Over-calling benign calcifications:** know the classic benign patterns (dermal, vascular, popcorn, lucent-centred); use magnification views and skin markers where needed.
- **Failing to close the loop:** description without category and plan is an incomplete report.
- **Not documenting limitations:** "Dense parenchyma limits sensitivity" and "Ultrasound limited by patient discomfort" are legitimate and important statements.

## Structured report templates and example phrases

These are starting points — adapt them to your local reporting system and the specific case.

**Screening normal:**
*"Two-view mammography, adequate positioning. No suspicious mass, calcifications, or distortion. Routine recall."*

**Screening recall decision:**
*"Left CC/MLO: new group of amorphous microcalcifications, UOQ, 7 cm FN, approximately 25 mm span — recall for assessment (magnification views and stereotactic biopsy likely required)."*

**Screening assessment outcome:**
*"Reason for recall: right UOQ calcifications. Magnification views confirm 32 mm segmental fine pleomorphic calcifications. DBT shows no associated mass. Provisional expectation: DCIS."*

**Symptomatic — reassuring:**
*"P2 discrete nodularity, 2 o'clock right breast. Mammography M1: no focal abnormality. Targeted ultrasound U1 at 2 o'clock, 3–6 cm FN: no focal lesion identified. Conclusion: benign nodularity. Reassure; advise to re-present if change."*

## Special scenarios

**Post-operative and post-radiotherapy breast:** expect skin thickening, trabecular oedema, seromas, and evolving fat necrosis. Describe these changes, note their evolution over time, and anchor clip positions. Be alert to new or increasing distortion or calcifications in the treated field — these may represent recurrence and should be categorised and biopsied where suspicious.

**Implants:** on mammography, document whether Eklund displacement views were obtained. On ultrasound, assess peri-implant fluid and capsule integrity. On MRI, use a structured lexicon distinguishing intracapsular rupture (linguine sign, keyhole sign) from extracapsular silicone. Note the implant location (subglandular versus subpectoral) as this affects the assessment technique.

**Male breast:** document whether any soft tissue change is symmetrical subareolar flame-shaped gynecomastia (benign) or an eccentric solid mass (suspicious). Assign M and U categories; recommend biopsy for any suspicious solid mass. Male breast cancer is rare but not negligible — do not dismiss it.

**Pregnancy and lactation:** prioritise ultrasound; avoid ionising radiation unless the clinical need outweighs the risk and this has been justified and documented. Note that increased vascularity and parenchymal density limit sensitivity. Do not withhold biopsy when imaging features are suspicious — image-guided core biopsy is safe in pregnancy.

**Neoadjuvant therapy response:** measure residual enhancing tumour in two orthogonal planes on MRI and on ultrasound, always referencing the biopsy clip. Comment on the pattern of response — concentric shrinkage (favourable for breast conservation) versus scattered residual foci (less favourable). Calcifications from DCIS often persist after treatment even when tumour has responded — describe their extent but do not interpret persisting calcifications as evidence of viable tumour without correlating histology.

## Quality, audit, and language

Consistent use of the M/U/B scoring systems enables meaningful audit — recall rates, PPVs by category, and interval cancer rates can only be measured if categories are applied consistently and recorded in a queryable way. Ambiguous or uncategorised reports break the audit chain.

Avoid language that hedges without adding information: "there may possibly be a subtle asymmetry which could perhaps warrant further evaluation" tells nobody anything. If you are uncertain, assign M3/U3, explain why, and recommend biopsy or short-interval follow-up with justification.

Treat peer review and double reading variance as learning, not criticism. Arbitration decisions should be recorded with rationale, not just outcome — this is how collective standards improve.

## Putting it together: a mental checklist

Before signing off any breast report, run through this:

- [ ] Indication and P-score documented?
- [ ] Prior imaging reviewed and explicitly compared?
- [ ] Technique and limitations stated?
- [ ] Lesion location, size, morphology, distribution, and associated features described?
- [ ] Axillary nodes assessed where relevant?
- [ ] Cross-modality correlation documented?
- [ ] Single, clear M and/or U category assigned per finding and overall?
- [ ] Action plan explicit and achievable?
- [ ] If biopsy performed: procedural details, clip position, and specimen radiography documented; histology addendum with B category and concordance to follow?
- [ ] Safety-netting provided if discharging?

Structured reporting is a scaffold, not a straitjacket. Use it to ensure completeness and clarity; apply judgement when findings do not fit neatly into a category. When you explain your reasoning in the report — why you called M4 rather than M3, why you regard the imaging and histology as discordant — you make the MDT more effective and your future self more accountable. That is what good radiology looks like.
